# Font Check

*Scripts > FreeLab > Font Check* holds three views of the current font: the languages its characters cover, its vertical metrics, and what keeps its masters from interpolating. Nothing in the font changes until one of its writing buttons is clicked: *Declare coverage* (Coverage), *Write suggested values* (Vertical metrics), or *Fix ticked glyphs* and *Fix this* (Masters). A declaration or a fix is one step of *Edit > Undo*; the vertical metrics are not in FontLab's History, and *Put back old values* takes them back. Its other buttons open Glyph Compose (which writes only when its own button is clicked), FontLab's Font Info, or a Glyph window.

Each view puts the answer first, then the action: a summary at the top, pictures where the matter is visual (characters, glyphs, lines), one line per thing with its details in a tooltip or on selection, and a few states shown as colored marks rather than sentences. The window keeps the conventions of FreeLab's other dialogs: a tab per view, a settings row and a top line with the counts in bold, grid lines between the cells of the lists, a log, a right-click on a glyph to flag it in FontLab (F3 and Shift+F3 go to the next and previous flagged row), *Show in Glyph window* (or a double-click), each view checked as its tab is first shown and again after an undo, a redo, or an edit in FontLab, and the dialog's ? button to explain any control. Nothing scrolls sideways: long texts are cut with an ellipsis and shown whole in their tooltips.

The window is laid out as every FreeLab window ([WINDOWS.md](WINDOWS.md#layout); the owner's round of November 17): the shown tab's status line under the top line, then the header's line (shown with no font too), then the tabs. Under the log, one row: at the left the shown tab's buttons that act on its lists (*Tick all*, *Untick all*, *Tick only what Glyph Compose can build*, *Build with Glyph Compose*, and *Copy glyphs* on *Coverage*; *Put back old values*, *Copy suggested values*, and *Open Font Info* on *Vertical metrics*; *Fix this* on *Masters*), then *Show in Glyph window*; at the right the shown tab's writing button (*Write suggested values*, *Fix ticked glyphs*), then *Cancel* and *Close*. Every caption has its colon (*Display:*, *Master:*, *Beside it:*), but *Compare every master with [Regular].*, a sentence, which ends with a period. A line the window logs about something the font lacks (the spaces, below) is amber, a warning (image 242), and *Glyph Compose* in a log line is a link that opens it in the window's place.

The three tabs' settings rows are as tall as one another, so what is under them never moves as a tab is shown (a row of a button alone is a few pixels shorter than a row with a spin box): each row stands in a widget of its own, whose least height is the tallest size hint of the controls in any of the rows, measured as the window is made and again at each switch (`level_rows`). With no font, nothing names the font's masters: *Compare every master with* reads a grayed *n/a*, not an empty box before its period (image 289), and the clipped glyphs' *Master:* and its dropdown are hidden until a check lists clipped glyphs of several masters (image 291, `show_no_masters`); the sentence beside the *Vertical metrics* tab's *Check* (*Every glyph measured in every master.*) says what *Check* does, so it is grayed with it, as a caption is with its control (image 292, `ak.caption`). The fields' placeholders end with an ellipsis (*Find a language, a character set, or a family…*, *Paste text or characters to check as a set of your own…*; *Name* stays as it is: image 309), and a tick's caption that is a sentence ends with its period (*Show the 48 scripts whose glyphs are fully missing from the font.*: image 324). Every count in the status lines and the log is bold with its noun, and with any adjective between them (*With **6 glyphs** ticked*, *missing at most **3 base characters***, *the **5 Legacy sets***, *Checked **2,512 languages**, **140 character sets**, and **172 scripts***, ***12 of the 2,000 glyphs** do not interpolate*, *all **4 masters***); a number with no noun of its own is bold alone (*the first **200** are*: images 297 and 301).

With a font, Font Check opens 1,400 pixels wide (or what the screen's free area allows, 40 pixels less, as the shared size is bounded), at the shared height. With no font, it opens at the other tools' size, and widens around its center when a font comes, unless the window was resized by hand (it, or any window of the session) or is maximized (`widen_for_font`). The width: at the other tools' 1000 pixels, the Coverage view's two lists stand too close, and the right one's last column is cut (*95 of 177 la…*). The characters' lists take 42% of the width to start with and the languages' lists the rest. The lists at the right fill their width exactly, and in a narrower window their columns give way in order. In the *Languages* and *Character sets* tabs the counts are always as wide as their contents (of the rows shown), and the names take the rest. In the *Families* tab, the long counts (*259 of 382 languages (85 within reach)*) give way first, cut with an ellipsis down to 120 pixels (whole in the row's tooltip); then the bars narrow, down to 48 pixels; then the names are cut, down to 90 pixels; only then does the bar close, and the counts after it. Live at 1,000 pixels, the counts kept their whole width while the names read *Austr…* and the bars were 20 pixels wide. The families' counts are set at the dialog's size, as the scripts' are, not in the list's enlarged font. Every column of those lists can be dragged (columns in Qt's *Stretch* and *ResizeToContents* modes cannot be, so none uses them): a column dragged keeps its width, and the names' column takes up the rest, or, when the names are the one dragged, the bar's column (or the last one), so the frame is never left with a gap (`column_widths`). A column dragged to the frame's edge, or past it, stops where the other columns keep their least (24 pixels at least, the shared module's least section; the names and the bar their own least). The families' bars are drawn in their cells to the column's width (a delegate, from counts the row holds), so a narrower column narrows them rather than cutting them. A tab bar too narrow for its tabs shows no scroll arrows: the tab chosen keeps its whole name and the others are cut with an ellipsis, each whole again once chosen (`tab_texts`).

## Coverage

Each language's orthography names the characters it is written with: its **base** characters (required in common text), its **auxiliary** ones (loan words, names, letters falling out of use), and the **marks** it needs on their own. The orthographies come from [Hyperglot](https://github.com/rosettatype/hyperglot) (Rosetta Type, Apache License 2.0) for the 799 languages it describes, and, for 1,713 languages it does not, from seven other open sources ([below](#the-languages-hyperglot-does-not-describe)), the last of them keyboard layouts; the declared sources and the layouts also give 66 of Hyperglot's languages a script Hyperglot does not give them in; each language's tooltip names its source, or every source it rests on. What it knows of and cannot check is counted too ([below](#what-font-check-cannot-check-yet)). A language is:

- **covered** (green) when the font has every base and auxiliary character and every mark;
- **within reach** (amber) when it misses at most the number of base characters set (*Within reach: missing at most 3 glyphs* to start with; a missing mark counts as one), or only auxiliary characters;
- **started** (red) when the font has at least one of its characters, but too few for it to be within reach;
- **missed** (gray) when the font has none of its characters.

The four colors are the same everywhere in the view: the scripts' bars, the dot before each language and character set, and the families' bars. *Covered* is green, not the palette's highlight color: the highlight is the selection's color and means nothing else in FreeLab, while green is already the color of a passed check and of the log's *done*. The four are mid tones, as the checks' icons are (64, 168, 92; 230, 150, 30; 222, 64, 52; and a gray at half strength), which read on the dark interface and on the light one; the log's own colors are text colors and come lighter on dark and darker on light, which filled marks do not need. *Started* and *missed* tell apart a language the font has some of the characters of from one it has none of.

The view has three parts.

- **The scripts**, the most languages first, each with a thin bar in the four colors and a count: *340 of 569 languages (60 within reach)*. *All scripts* comes first. Clicking a script shows only its languages below. The list is six rows tall at most, and a short window (a screen 480 pixels tall) may take it down to two rows, scrolled. A check box under the list, *Show the 48 scripts whose glyphs are fully missing from the font.* (its caption carries the count), lists the scripts the font has none of too (fewer than five of their letters, and no language covered); it is ticked to start with.
- **The glyphs to add**, at the left, in two tabs. The interface says *glyph* for what the font has or would get (a row of these lists, what is ticked, built, copied, or opened), and keeps *character* for what Unicode or a source defines: a character set, a code point, an orthography's base and auxiliary characters.
  - ***High-value missing glyphs (N)***, with the count of its rows: what the languages within reach need, one row per glyph (a capital and its small letter together when both are missing, since a language needs them together), drawn large (a missing glyph cannot be drawn from the font), with its character's short Unicode name, its code points, **+N**, and the languages' names. *+N* counts the languages the row completes on its own; a row that completes none on its own (its languages miss other glyphs too) shows *+0* in gray and names what else they miss: *Wolof (with ɓ)*. Rows are ranked by *+N*, then by how many languages need them.
  - **The selection's own tab**, grayed and named *Select language, character set, or family* until a language, a character set (a script of Unicode is one), or a family is selected at the right, then named after it with the count of glyphs it misses (*Wolof (2)*): its missing glyphs and nothing else. Its help and its tooltip name the three things it works on. Those the first tab ranks come first, as they are there; the glyphs no row there has (a started or missed language's, a character set's, a language of a script the font lacks) get rows of their own, the first 200 of them, and a line over the list counts the rest. For a family, the rows are what its languages not covered miss, those that complete the most of them first.
  - Ticking rows updates the line above the tabs: *With 6 glyphs ticked: +14 languages (Latin)*. It counts sets, not sums: a language counts once every glyph it needs is ticked, so the line is exact whatever rows are ticked. A glyph ticked in one tab is ticked in the other. The buttons, in the window's bottom row while *Coverage* is shown, act on the tab of glyphs shown: *Tick all* ticks every row it lists (the ticks of rows it does not list are kept), *Untick all* clears every tick, and *Tick only what Glyph Compose can build* ticks the rows listed with a glyph Glyph Compose can build and unticks the rest; each is grayed out, with a tooltip saying why, when it would change nothing. What a language needs is its missing base characters and marks (Hyperglot's own notion of support); a language missing only auxiliary characters needs those, and its rows say so in their tooltips. *Build with Glyph Compose* (*Build this glyph with Glyph Compose* or *Build those glyphs with Glyph Compose* once some are ticked or a row selected, the count in its tooltip: *Build 2 glyphs* for one row selected, a capital and its small letter, read as two rows) opens Glyph Compose on the ticked glyphs of the tab shown that it can build (asked once per check): letters with diacritics on its *Diacritics* tab, from the font's letters and marks, and other glyphs (spaces, dashes, quotes, look-alikes...) on its *Glyph generation* tab, each tab listing them alone; it opens on the tab with more and says what the other holds, and a row's tooltip says which tab builds its glyph. A draft of Glyph generation (a starting point) does not count as buildable. The button is unavailable, with a tooltip saying why, when Glyph Compose can build none of them. *Copy glyphs* (*Copy this glyph*, *Copy those glyphs*) copies the ticked glyphs of the tab shown (or the selected row's), as text. The shared module grays such buttons when the first of the lists nearest them has no check box; the two tabs' lists are equally near, and Qt's stack puts the list it shows last, so the list shown is put first (`left_page`). In the bottom row every list is as near the buttons, so the lists' halves are made a child of the page before the scripts' list (which has no check boxes): the shown list of glyphs is the first the shared module finds.
  - A double-click on a row opens that row's glyph in a Glyph window, whatever is ticked. *Show in Glyph window* opens the ticked glyphs of the tab shown, or the selected row's when none is ticked; its tooltip says which.
  - **Spaces**: a no-break space (U+00A0) or a narrow no-break space (U+202F) the font lacks is a row of *High-value missing glyphs* where a language it covers or nearly covers groups the thousands of its numbers with it (*12 345*), as Unicode CLDR 49 records them (*checks/space_conventions.json*, by *checks/train/spaces.py*: French, Esperanto, Romansh, Venetian, and Anii with U+202F; Russian, Polish, Czech, Swedish, Ukrainian, and 55 more with U+00A0). It completes no language (their letters decide that), and its tooltip says why it is listed. The other spaces the font lacks (those no row lists, then the figure, thin, hair, en, and em spaces) are named in one line of the log after each check (once a font, again when it changes), with where Glyph Compose builds them: amber, since it says what the font lacks (image 242). *Glyph Compose* in that line is a link of the window's own (`freelab-spaces:` and the spaces' code points, which the log hands to `log_link`): clicked, it hands those spaces to Glyph Compose as *Build with Glyph Compose* hands glyphs, so Glyph Compose opens on its *Glyph generation* tab with them listed alone and ticked, and the log says so (`build_spaces`).
- **The lists at the right**, in three tabs whose captions count what each lists: *Languages*, *Character sets*, and *Families*. A field finds a language by its name, autonym, code, or one of its other names, a character set by its name, or a family by its name; a choice keeps all, or the covered, within reach, started, or missed ones; and the order is *Within reach first* (the list's own: the fewest characters missing first, then the covered ones, then the others), *Most covered first*, *Least covered first*, *A to Z*, or *Z to A* (by English name, a letter with a diacritic with its letter). The three act on the tab shown, and each tab keeps its list as it was, scrolled where it was and its headings and families as they were opened, as the tabs are switched and as a search is cleared ([below](#searching-and-switching-tabs)). The field has a cross that clears it.
  - ***Languages***: the languages under their scripts' headings. Each row has a colored dot (its state), its name and autonym, and, within reach, its missing glyphs in short (*ß*, or *Ǎ ǎ +1*); a language covered thanks to the anchors says *composed*, one whose characters were inferred from a text says *inferred*, and one read from a keyboard layout says *keyboard*. Its tooltip says everything: its source (Hyperglot's status and validity, or the other source's name, *declared*, *inferred from text*, or *from a keyboard layout*, its draft level, and its status), the base, auxiliary, and mark characters missing (the first 60 of each list, then *and 3,412 more*, and where they are all listed), the ones composed, and its family, a paragraph each with a blank line between two. A click on a script's heading closes it, and another opens it (an arrow drawn before the heading shows which; a double-click does the same, once); the click does nothing else: Qt does not see it, so the heading is neither selected nor made current, and what was selected stays so. What is closed is kept by the heading's script through refills, other orders, and searches.
  - The headings are one per script of Unicode. Hyperglot labels some orthographies with names that are no script of their own: Plains Cree with *Cree*, Eastern Canadian Inuktitut with *Inuktitut Syllabics*, and Ojibwa with *Ojibwe Syllabics*, all in Unicode's Canadian Aboriginal script; Mandarin with *Chinese*, Japanese with *Kanji*, and Korean with *Hanja*. They come under *Canadian Syllabics* (Hyperglot's own name for that script) and *Han* (`SCRIPT_GROUPS`), in the scripts' list, its bars and counts, the headings, and the line of languages gained alike; the other labels are one per Unicode script already, some under another name than Unicode's (*Burmese* for Myanmar, *Geʽez* for Ethiopic), and are kept. A row keeps its source's label where it informs, in its tooltip: *Japanese (日本語), Han script (Kanji)*. A language with orthographies in several scripts is listed under each (Pontic Greek under Cyrillic, Greek, and Latin; Sanskrit under Devanagari and Nandinagari): each row's tooltip says so, *Also listed under Greek and Latin: it has an orthography in each of those scripts too.*
  - ***Character sets***: the sets that are no languages (below), under their nine groups, then *Scripts (Unicode)*, every script Unicode encodes as a set of its own ([below](#every-script-of-unicode-as-a-set)). A field under the list takes text or characters pasted: they are checked as a set of the user's own ([below](#a-set-of-your-own)).
  - ***Families***: the languages under their families and branches, as [Glottolog](https://glottolog.org) classifies them, each family and branch with a bar in the four colors and the same count as a script (*155 of 180 languages (12 within reach)*). A branch that holds a single branch is skipped, and one that holds a single language gives way to it, so every node listed tells things apart (Glottolog's tree is up to 22 levels deep for these languages). The languages Glottolog calls isolates come under *Language isolates*, and those it does not classify under a last node, *Languages Glottolog does not classify*, whose tooltip says why. Families are closed to start with: a click on a family's arrow opens or closes it and selects nothing (nor does a double-click on the arrow), and a double-click on its name opens or closes it too; a search of three characters or more opens every family it lists (a letter or two match most languages: opening them all makes some 4,300 rows). A click on a family's name selects it. A language written in two scripts is listed twice, each row saying its script. Beside each family's bar, in gray, a fourth column counts the languages of the family that cannot be checked yet (*885 not yet covered*), and the family's last node, *Not yet covered (885)*, lists them ([below](#what-font-check-cannot-check-yet)).

The lists are linked. Clicking a language, a character set, or a family checks nothing but opens the second tab of glyphs on what it misses. Clicking another puts its glyphs in their place; clicking the selected row again, or an empty part of the list, unselects it, grays that tab again, and shows the high-value list. A click on an empty part of a list of glyphs clears its selection too (Qt keeps a single selection whatever is clicked). The selection goes with the tab at the right when the tab shown lists it too (a language selected under its script is selected under its family, which opens to show it). The ticks are kept through all of this, and a started or missed language counts in the line of languages gained once every glyph it needs is ticked. Double-clicking a language or a character set opens its glyphs in a Glyph window (the first 200), where the missing ones show as FontLab's placeholders.

The rows of a closed heading or family are made when it is opened, not before. With Hyperglot's 829 orthographies the *Families* tab made 1,376 rows at each filling (a row per family, branch, and language, most of them behind closed families); with 2,507 it would make 4,168, and makes 163, one per family. The *Languages* tab opens its scripts to start with, so it makes a row per language listed: 2,597 with every script (Latin alone has 2,046), against 881 before; closing a script leaves its rows unmade at the next filling. A language selected under a heading that is then closed stays selected, without a row, and gets its row, marked selected, when the heading is opened again.

The *Languages* tab shows the languages of the scripts listed above it: all of them to start with, or the one clicked. A search looks through every language all the same, so *lao* finds Lao when only the Latin script is shown: what it finds outside the list comes under a heading that says why it is not listed otherwise (*Lao (1): a script the font has none of*, *Greek (2): not the script chosen above*, *Historical languages (1): left out by the Display choice above*), and a line under the list counts what matched, or, without a search, the languages not listed and why. A find that the choice of covered, within reach, started, or missed holds back is counted there too.

### What the font info declares

A check compares the font info's Unicode ranges and code pages (OS/2) with what the glyphs cover, by the rules the tools keep them with after their writes ([WINDOWS.md](WINDOWS.md#the-font-info)). Where they differ, the log says how, once a font (*The font info's coverage differs from the glyphs: Unicode ranges (not declared: Hangul Syllables; declared, without a glyph of it: Cyrillic); code pages (not declared: Latin 1 (1252)).*), and names the Coverage tab's *Declare coverage* button (grayed otherwise), which sets both fields to what the glyphs cover. The code pages FreeLab leaves as declared (Macintosh Roman, the MS-DOS ones, OEM, Symbol) are kept. It is one step of FontLab's History: unlike the vertical metrics, these two fields are part of what a step records, so *Edit > Undo* takes them back (found live). FontLab 7 exports them as the font info holds them, never recalculated. After a window's first check, the log says once where the kerning is checked, with a link that opens *Kerning Check* (image 391).

### Other names, and languages no source describes

A language goes by several names, and the list shows one. A search finds it by any of the names Glottolog records for it (30,457 of them, for 2,399 of the 2,512 languages): *Nama* finds Khoekhoe, *Allemand* German, and case and diacritics are set aside (*francais*). The row found keeps its one name; its tooltip says *Found by its other name Nama.*, and the line under the list says so too. The search stays a single test per language at each keystroke: each language's own names and its other names are folded once into two texts, and each short text folded (the text typed, a family's name) is kept, since folding them anew would fold some 25,000 texts at each keystroke.

Hyperglot describes the languages someone has documented an orthography for: 800 of them, of which this data keeps 799, out of the 7,861 individual languages ISO 639-3 identifies (7,928 codes with the macrolanguages and special codes, tables of 2026-10-15), about one in ten. *checks/train/languages.py* filters nothing of the kind: it leaves out only the six macrolanguage entries that have no orthography of their own (Dogri, Fulah, Konkani, Lahnda, Nepali, Zaza) and Ter Sami, whose one orthography Hyperglot marks secondary. Juǀʼhoan (ISO 639-3 *ktz*, Glottolog's South-Eastern Ju, of the Kxa family) is not among them, nor any other Ju or ǃKung variety; it is checked from Google Fonts' language data, and found by either name. With the other sources, Font Check checks 2,512 languages, about three in ten.

So that a search for one of the others does not just come back empty, the data lists the 5,035 languages with an ISO 639-3 code that Glottolog has and no source describes (nor any through its dialects or members: Latvian is described through Standard Latvian), with their other names and the family each belongs to, and the line under the list names those a search fits, the first six of them: *huc* gives *Glottolog also lists Amkoe (huc), which none of Font Check's sources documents an orthography for: Font Check cannot check it.* A code or a whole name comes first, then a whole other name, then the names and other names that hold the text; a text of one or two characters fits a code or a whole name only (*juu* is the code of Ju, a Chadic language of Nigeria, which is what a web search for "Juu" also brings up).

### What Font Check cannot check yet

Font Check keeps count of what it knows of and has no data for, so that the gap is seen and can be filled. Counted on 2026-11-14:

| Known | Checked | Not yet covered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7,861 languages Glottolog 5.4 lists with an ISO 639-3 code | 2,512, all under Glottolog's families (Minang placed by hand beside Nyunga) | 5,035, in 226 families (the difference is the entries Glottolog files as retired or spurious, never offered, codes the two counts treat differently, macrolanguages and dialects, and 18 languages described through their dialects or members, Latvian, Serbian-Croatian-Bosnian, and Akan among them) |
| 240 families (with the isolates as one) | 163 have a language that is checked | 77 have none |
| 172 scripts in Unicode 17.0.0 (*Scripts.txt*, without *Common* and *Inherited*) | 112 have an orthography written in them; all 172 are checked as scripts | 60 have no orthography: the script's whole repertoire is their measure (58 not yet covered, and 2 notations, Braille and SignWriting, which are character sets) |

The keyboard layouts ([below](#keyboard-layouts)) give 18 languages, and an orthography to 16 scripts: Adlam, Ahom, Bassa Vah, Batak, Cham, Gunjala Gondi, Gurung Khema, Lepcha, Masaram Gondi, Sora Sompeng, Tai Yo, Takri, Wancho, Warang Citi, Yezidi, and Meetei Mayek, which is also SLDR's declared orthography of Manipuri. Balinese, Deseret, Javanese, Nandinagari, Shavian, and Sundanese have theirs as declared second scripts of Hyperglot's languages ([below](#the-languages-hyperglot-does-not-describe)), and Lisu and Mandaic from counted texts whose script is found by Unicode's own code. SLDR's Mongolian in the Mongolian script (*mn_Mong*), whose code is the macrolanguage's while Hyperglot describes its member Halh Mongolian in Cyrillic, is filed under Peripheral Mongolian (*mvf*), the one member CLDR's likely subtags write in that script and region.

- **In the *Families* tab**, each family counts, in gray beside its bar, the languages of it that cannot be checked (Austronesian 880, Atlantic-Congo 862, Sino-Tibetan 337, Indo-European 307), its tooltip names four of them and the total, and its last node, *Not yet covered (N)*, lists them all, A to Z, its rows made when it is opened. A family with nothing else is listed for them alone. Each row is there to be read, not selected: its tooltip says what the language is, that no source says which characters it is written with, the script a source records for it where one does, and its other names. A search finds these languages by code, name, or other name, opens the node (up to 50 rows), and the line under the list names them as above. Choosing one script above, or one level, leaves them out.
- **In the *Languages* tab**, a closing section, *Scripts not yet covered (58)*, closed until it is clicked, lists the scripts Unicode encodes that no orthography here is written in (Glagolitic, Phoenician, Old Turkic, and others); a search that names one opens it. Their previews are drawn in FreeLang, and *not yet covered* means only that no source here describes a language written in the script. The heading's tooltip says so first: *Not yet covered means that none of Font Check's sources describes a language written in the script, not that the script is unknown or cannot be drawn: Unicode encodes it, its glyphs are drawn in FreeLang (or the system's fonts, for the largest scripts), and the font is checked against its whole repertoire.*
- **Braille and SignWriting are not among them.** They can never be covered by those criteria: they are character sets, not languages. Their characters do not depend on the language written (Braille's 256 cells are the same code points whatever the language or contraction code; SignWriting's symbols are one set for every sign language), so no language's character list, and no count of languages, can rank a font for them. They are checked as character sets: *Braille Patterns (Unicode block)* and *Sutton SignWriting (Unicode block)* under *Notation*, and *Braille* and *SignWriting* under *Scripts (Unicode)*, whose tooltips say why. A search for one in the *Languages* tab says so under the list, the set's name a link that shows it in the *Character sets* tab; as for the known gaps, the search is the whole name, or part of one from three characters on. They stand in one constant, `NOTATION_SCRIPTS`, each with its reason. Every other script not yet covered wrote one or more languages, so a source could describe it: Duployan is a shorthand, but also the everyday script of Chinook Wawa and several Salishan languages (its block is a *Notation* set too, which answers another question); Bopomofo is a closed phonetic set for Mandarin and Taiwanese; Phags Pa, Zanabazar Square, Soyombo, Siddham, Grantha, and Khitan Small Script each wrote languages. Deseret and Shavian are orthographies of English, counted as English's rows. Each row of the section is the script checked as a set ([below](#every-script-of-unicode-as-a-set)): a dot for its level, *167 of 167 missing*, a tooltip that says *No language data exists for it yet: none of Font Check's sources gives an orthography written in it, so the script's whole repertoire is the measure.*, and a click that hands its missing glyphs to the left as a language's. The choice of covered, within reach, started, or missed holds for them too. The heading's tooltip says what cannot be checked at all (below).
- **The line under the list** says what can be checked of what is known (*Font Check can check 2,512 of the 7,861 languages Glottolog lists with an ISO 639-3 code, written in 112 of Unicode's 172 scripts (58 not yet covered, and 2 notations checked as character sets). Every script of Unicode is checked as a set of its own, under Character sets.*), what limits the rest, with its date (*As of November 2026, of the 5,035 languages not covered, 4,500 have a script on record but no source that gives their characters, and 535 have no script on record (many have no written form, or no open description that can be cited). The limit is the record itself, not the tool.*, the counts taken from the data files as the line is written), and how to add to it: *These languages are known, but FreeLab has no data on how they are written. To add one, open an issue on FreeLab's repository with the characters the language uses and a source FreeLab can reuse (under an open license, or in the public domain).* The word *issue* is the link to the repository's issues; a tooltip that says the same, where nothing can be clicked, writes the address out. It is the script's one constant `REPOSITORY`.

A contribution needs: the language's ISO 639-3 code; its script; its base characters (capitals and small letters where the script has both); the combining marks it needs on their own; its auxiliary characters, if any (loan words, names); a source that can be cited (a published orthography, a standard, a grammar, a dictionary); and that source's license, or a statement that the list of letters is the contributor's own. A list of letters is a fact; a copied table or text is not.

#### What cannot be supported, as of November 2026

Two limits are not Font Check's to lift, and the window and this page say so plainly, with the date. The sources in use, those weighed and left out, and those to look at again when the data is updated (the Script Encoding Initiative's list of scripts not yet encoded among them) are registered in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md).

- **A script Unicode has not encoded can be neither checked nor built**, except through private-use code points, which mean what one font says they mean. Unicode's own list of them ([Unsupported Scripts](https://www.unicode.org/standard/unsupported.html), read again on 2026-11-13; the page carries no date of its own) names, under *Modern Scripts*, Loma and Naxi Dongba (Moso), and, under *Archaic and Obsolete Scripts*, Balti-A and Balti-B, Chola, Landa, Maya Hieroglyphs, Numidian, Pahlavi (Book), Pyu, Ranjana, and Satavahana. No name is added here from elsewhere. The interface names the same twelve in the tooltip of *Scripts not yet covered* (the script's `NOT_ENCODED`).
- **Of the 5,035 languages not covered, the limit is the record itself.** Counted from the data files on 2026-11-14: for **4,500** a source records a script (Latin for 3,969 of them) and no source gives the characters, the largest group by far; for **535** no script is on record, of which **156** are sign languages. For many languages of both groups no written form is in use, or no open description that can be cited says how the language is written: a corpus to count or an orthography to quote does not exist to be found. Where one exists and is missing here, it can be added ([above](#what-font-check-cannot-check-yet)); where none does, no tool can check a font for the language, and a pasted alphabet ([below](#a-set-of-your-own)) is the way.

What is known of the 5,035 that cannot be checked:

- **Their script.** For 4,500 of them (89%), Unicode CLDR 49 (the scripts of its language data, and its likely scripts) or Google Fonts' language data records a script without giving characters: Latin for 3,969, Devanagari for 116, Arabic for 115, Cyrillic for 31, Tibetan for 30, Chinese characters for 27, Burmese for 23, Thai for 21, and 67 other scripts for a few each (19 languages have two or more). A script recorded is not an orthography in use: CLDR's likely script is often the script a language would be written in. The other 535 have no script recorded here: 156 are sign languages, and many of the rest are spoken languages with no written form in use. How many of the world's languages are written at all is not established here: Ethnologue's figures are the usual citation and could not be read (its pages redirect to a paywall).
- **Their characters exist in Unicode, with few exceptions.** A language written in Latin, Cyrillic, Arabic, or another encoded script uses encoded letters, and a letter Unicode has no single character for is a letter and combining marks (the data holds 0 private-use code points in 2,644 orthographies). The exceptions are scripts not encoded yet: Unicode's own list ([As Yet Unsupported Scripts](https://www.unicode.org/standard/unsupported.html), read on 2026-11-13) names Loma and Naxi Dongba among the modern ones, and Balti-A and Balti-B, Chola, Landa, Maya hieroglyphs, Numidian, Book Pahlavi, Pyu, Ranjana, and Satavahana among the historic ones, with more on its roadmaps; its [pipeline](https://www.unicode.org/alloc/Pipeline.html) (updated 2026-11-02) holds the scripts accepted for coming versions. Until a script is encoded, fonts for it use the Private Use Area by a convention of their own (SIL's PUA assignments, the ConScript Unicode Registry): Font Check checks nothing there.
- **The character sets cover most of them without naming them.** Of the 2,084 Latin orthographies the data had before the keyboard layouts, *Adobe Latin 5* holds every base character of 2,067 (99%), and Google's Latin sets together (*GF Latin Core*, *Plus*, *Vietnamese*, *PriAfrican*, *African*, *Beyond*) of 1,989 (95%; all but three characters at most of 2,071). Google's Cyrillic sets hold every base character of 87 of 98 Cyrillic orthographies (*Adobe Cyrillic 3* of 65), and its Arabic sets of 44 of 78 (all but three at most of 71). So a font that covers those sets very likely draws an unlisted language of the script, though Font Check cannot say which: the tooltip of a language not yet covered names the sets of its recorded script as the nearest measure.

#### Known gaps, and fonts to consider

*checks/coverage_gaps.json* (made by *checks/train/coverage_gaps.py*; [COVERAGE-GAPS.md](COVERAGE-GAPS.md) is its account for people) lists what Font Check knows of and does not check, and fonts to consider for what FreeLang cannot draw. Font Check reads it when it reads its other data, and does without it.

- **Known gaps** (Taiwanese Hokkien and Hakka characters, Korean gukja, Bai characters, Jurchen and Khitan Large Script, emoji sequences, ideographic and standardized variation sequences): a search that names one, by its name or another (*ZWJ sequences*, *IVD*), says so under the list in any tab, as it names the languages Glottolog lists and no source describes: *Font Check knows of Korean gukja, which it does not check: no Unihan field or source marks them.* A text of one or two characters must be a whole name.
- **Fonts to consider**: a set's or a script's tooltip names the fonts listed for it, their license, and that each is a separate download, nothing more: *Fonts to consider for the rest: Plangothic P1, Plangothic P2 (SIL OFL 1.1, a separate download).* (Han, whose 103,351 ideographs no font file can hold; with Noto Sans SC, JP, or KR they draw every one), and Noto Color Emoji for the emoji blocks (their color forms and sequences). FreeLab ships none of them.

#### A set of your own

For a language no source describes, a designer finds its alphabet (the language's Wikipedia or Omniglot page, ScriptSource, an SIL or academic description) and pastes it, or a text in the language, in the field under the *Character sets* tab. The characters are checked as a set, *Pasted characters*, listed first under *Your sets* and selected: each character once, in NFC, spaces, controls, and format characters left out, a letter with a combining mark that has no single character kept together. Its missing glyphs go to the second tab at the left as any set's do, to check, copy, open in a Glyph window, or build with Glyph Compose. *Save set* keeps it under the name given, in FreeLab's settings folder (*font_check_sets.json* in *%LOCALAPPDATA%\FreeLab*), not in the font or the repository; *Remove set* removes the saved set selected. The sets of one's own take no part in the top line's count of character sets. The two fields read *Paste text or characters to check as a set of your own…* and *Name* until they are filled.

With FontLab alone, the same work is: *Font > Generate Glyphs* by Unicode name or code point for the letters, then anchors and components for the letters with diacritics. With FreeLab: the set above says what is missing, Glyph Compose builds the precomposed letters and places the marks' anchors, and Font Check counts a letter the anchors compose. A private-use code point is needed only for a character Unicode does not have; such a glyph is listed by its name, drawn from its outlines, and checked by nothing here.

### Tooltips

Every tooltip of the window is rich text, which Qt wraps: a tree row's plain-text tooltip is laid out on one line, a few thousand pixels wide for a character many languages need. Its width follows the window's: a tooltip whose longest line would pass about half the window's width, between 360 and 900 pixels (and never wider than the window less 40), is given that width; one whose longest line is shorter but past 400 pixels is given that line's width; a shorter one is left to its own. The paragraphs of a tooltip are a blank line apart, and a list of characters is cut at its first 60 (*… and 3,412 more*, then *Select it to list its missing glyphs at the left.*), a list of languages at its first 30: uncut, Mandarin Chinese's tooltip is a wall of ideographs the height of the screen, 5,167 characters of text; cut, it is 728. The tooltips that name languages or characters (the lists' rows, the families, the line of languages gained, *Show in Glyph window*'s) are set in *FreeLang* (below), which has every name, autonym, and orthography character; in Qt's own font, an autonym or a missing character of a script the interface font lacks shows as boxes. The *What's This* texts are left to Qt, which wraps them itself, rich or plain; none was seen wider than the window.

A tooltip's lines are not measured in a font: each character counts 7 pixels, 14 for a wide one (ideographs, kana, Hangul syllables, Tangut, fullwidth forms, by a table of ranges, so that the same text gets the same width under FontLab's Python 2.7, whose Unicode data is 5.2, as under Python 3). Measuring a line in the dialog's font freezes the window for seconds when the font lacks its characters (Duployan's, for one): Qt then looks through the system's fonts for one that has them (the same search the lists' rows are spared by making their tooltips when asked for, [below](#the-progress-bar-and-the-tooltips-made-when-asked-for)). Under the tests' fakes every tooltip, the largest rows' included (Mandarin Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Duployan, Han, Hangul, Tangut, SignWriting, the Math property's set, *Adobe Latin 5*, the largest families), is made in at most 1.2 ms. A row's tooltip is made once per check and width, and given again each time the mouse rests on the row.

### Names and characters in FreeLang

Every widget that can show a name or a character of any script is set in FreeLang: the lists at the right (larger), the two glyphs' lists (whose *Languages* column names what else a language misses), the line over them (the languages gained, the selection's name), the selection's tab's caption (a set of one's own is named in any script), the search field (an autonym typed), the field of a set of one's own and its name, and the line under the lists (the other names a search found languages by). So are the tooltips that name them, and the log's lines that quote characters or a set's name (*Copied …*, *Diacritics lists …*, *Saved the set …*) set them in FreeLang too. What stays in the dialog's font is what is English and digits only: the scripts' names and counts, the top line, the buttons, the captions of the tabs at the right, and the other two views (glyph names are ASCII by convention).

### Searching and switching tabs

Measured with the real data under the tests' fakes (2,511 languages checked, the families, the 5,036 unlisted names), the Python side of a keystroke takes 30 to 100 ms in any tab; what costs seconds in FontLab is Qt's making rows, some 2,600 for a whole list. So rows are made as rarely as can be:

- Each tab at the right has two lists, the full one and a search's. A search lists its finds in the second; the first stays as it is behind it, so clearing the search shows it again at once, scrolled where it was, with no row made. A list is filled anew only when what it shows would change (a check, another level, order, script chosen above, or search, a heading or family opened or closed elsewhere); otherwise switching to its tab shows it as it was, scrolled where it was and with its headings as they were opened, and its selection follows as a refill would have found it (the row selected and scrolled to, or none).
- A search waits for a pause of 250 ms in the typing before it lists its finds, so typing *mandarin* lists once, not eight times. Clearing the field, by its cross or by deleting the text, is not delayed.
- A search of one or two characters does not open the families it lists: *m* makes 182 rows, where opening every family would make 4,328 (*man*, 1,288 rows, opens them).

Rows made, keystroke by keystroke (*m*, *ma*, *man*, *mand*, *manda*), then clearing, then switching away and back: *Languages*, 1,856, 1,095, 262, 49, 27, then 0 to clear and 0 to come back; *Character sets*, 116, 38, 6, 2, 2, then 0 and 0; *Families*, 182, 133, 1,288, 297, 117, then 0 and 0. The time Qt takes per row could not be measured without FontLab.

Timed offline with the tests' fakes on the code points of Noto Sans (2,297 glyphs: 55 scripts listed, 1,587 languages under all of them, 1,456 under Latin) and of Noto Sans JP (16,088 glyphs), the Python side of a click on a script takes 1 to 40 ms; the rows the click makes are the cost. The glyphs' list at the left (213 rows for every script, 160 for Latin) is made anew at each click; the lists at the right are kept:

- Each tab at the right has five lists: every script's, the last three scripts chosen's, and a search's (`VIEW_MODES`). A click on *All scripts* shows every script's list as it was, scrolled where it was, with no row made, and so does a click on one of the last three scripts chosen (unless what it shows changed since: a check, the level, the order, a heading opened or closed); a fourth script takes the list shown least recently. So a click from Latin to Cyrillic makes Cyrillic's 103 rows, and back to Latin, none.
- The Character sets tab is never made anew for a script's click.
- A click that makes 300 rows or more (`LISTING_BUSY_ROWS`: All scripts to Latin, on Noto Sans) shows the window's progress bar in its top row, with what it lists beside it (*Listing the languages written in Latin…*), until the lists are shown. It can be stopped (the tools menu, the arrows, Close, Esc: [WINDOWS.md](WINDOWS.md)): the rows listed so far stay, and the list is made anew next time.

Rows made per click on Noto Sans, under the fakes (the right list, then the glyphs' list): *All scripts* to Latin, 1,456 and 160; Latin to *All scripts*, 0 and 213; Latin to Cyrillic, 103 and 50. Live (the coordinator's pass, Noto Sans): *All scripts* to Latin 0.15 s, to Cyrillic 0.011 s, back to *All scripts* 0.046 s with no row made.

**The character sets, counted one way.** The top line, the log, the *Character sets* tab, and Proof's summary take their counts from one function, `set_tally`: the sets counted (140 in the data), Unicode's scripts each checked as a set and counted apart (172), the *Legacy* sets (5, wholly optional, listed and never counted), and the user's own (listed, not counted). The tab's caption counts its rows, as the other tabs' captions do, and its tooltip adds them up (*317 rows: 140 character sets counted in the line above, 172 scripts of Unicode, each checked as a set (counted apart), and 5 Legacy sets, wholly optional (not counted)*); the top line says the Legacy sets are not counted; Proof gives the same count as the top line (*33 of 140* on Noto Sans), and the scripts a line of their own.

**A glyph's name in the high-value list.** A character FontLab's Python cannot name (its Unicode data is 5.2: U+A7CB is of 13.0) shows its code point once. A name keeps the words that say how the letter is drawn (*circled a*, *full-width A*) while the script's name goes (*r with caron*). Names for the 1,331 characters of the orthographies that Unicode 5.2 lacks would take a data file of some 50 KB, made with Python 3's Unicode data and checked against FontLab's 2.7: not done.

### What is grayed or hidden when there is nothing to act on

Before a check has listed anything (no font open, or the first check under way), the search field, the level, the order, the tabs of both sides, and the fields of a set of one's own are grayed, and the scripts' list and its check box (*Show the N scripts …*) are hidden until the check fills them; the *Check* button stays, since it is how a font opened since is read. During a check, the settings and the view's tabs are grayed, and the lists' area is blank under the progress bar. With nothing selected, the selection's tab stays grayed, and each button over the glyphs says in its tooltip why it is grayed. An empty search shows an empty list with the line under it saying so; the level and the order still act. In *Vertical metrics*, the line-spacing choice and its share are grayed until there are values to suggest, and the master shown among the clipped glyphs is hidden with a single master; in *Masters*, the masters to compare with are grayed until there is a choice. To check in FontLab: that nothing else is clickable without a font (the *Glyph window* and font info buttons are grayed too).

The window follows fonts, as FreeLab's other windows do (the shared module's `fonts_tick`): it closes with its font; opened with no font open, every view is empty (the lists, the diagram, the masters' glyphs), the top line says *No font is open. This window takes the next font you open.*, and the controls above are grayed; when a font opens, the view shown is checked as at opening and the others when shown (`font_changed`, from a timer: nothing escapes it, and a check under way is left to finish). The shared module also clears a list's selection on a click beside it; the scripts' list and the lists at the right keep theirs (they carry the `freelab_keep_selection` property: their selection feeds the rest, and they unselect on a click in their empty part themselves), while the glyphs' lists are left to it (Font Check clears them on such a click too: clearing twice is clearing once).

A character the font lacks as one glyph still counts when the font can **compose** it: Unicode's decomposition (NFD) gives its letter and combining marks, the font has them, and the letter has an anchor each mark attaches to (*top* on the letter, *_top* on the mark), so the font's mark feature puts them together. The longest part the font has is used, so *ḗ* stands on the font's *ē* with an acute. A character given decomposed (Hyperglot writes some letters as a base and marks) counts when the font has its composed form (NFC). A single character never counts through another one Unicode calls the same (a canonical singleton): the letters with oxia need their own glyphs or their letter and mark, not those with tonos (they are required in their own right), nor do the ohm, Kelvin, and ångström signs count through *Ω*, *K*, and *Å*, or a compatibility ideograph through its unified one. Nothing past U+FFFF is normalized, in the tests either: FontLab's Python 2.7 is a narrow build that normalizes none of it. A letter and a mark the font has but no anchor joins are missing, and the row's tooltip says the font has their parts; unticking *Count letters its anchors compose.* counts only the font's own glyphs. Hangul syllables do not compose from jamo here (jamo are letters, not marks), nor modifier letters from their base letters.

*Display*, in the settings row, chooses the languages counted and listed: *All languages* to start with, by their status, which the data keeps for each orthography (*Living and constructed*: Esperanto in, Latin and Ancient Greek out; *Living only*; *Historical only*, no native speakers left), or *High-value only* ([below](#high-value-languages)). Hyperglot gives each of its languages a status (795 orthographies living, 23 historical, 11 constructed). The other sources say less: an orthography is historical where Google Fonts' data says so (42), constructed where Glottolog files the language under its *Artificial Language* family (1: N’Ko, which Glottolog files there), and has no status given otherwise (1,773; a keyboard layout gives none); a language of no given status counts with the living ones, and its tooltip says *status not given*.

*Languages from texts and keyboards*, beside it, is ticked to start with: unticked, the orthographies no source declares are left out of the counts and the lists (804 rows: 767 inferred from a text and 37 read from a keyboard layout, for 772 languages that have no declared row, and 7 second scripts of languages that do), and a search still finds them, under a heading of the same words. The top line counts them either way: *Of those covered or within reach, 12 are inferred from text or read from a keyboard layout, not declared by a source (their rows say so).*, or *804 languages from texts and keyboards are left out.* (the count is of rows, as the lists'). A keyboard layout is no source's declaration either, and a second check box for 37 rows would crowd the settings row, so the one box holds both kinds, its *What's This* saying what each is and how its rows say so. A keyboard layout's tooltip says *from a keyboard layout* after the source's name, and its second line: *From a keyboard layout (Language Tools, Apache License 2.0): the characters its keys and rules produce, not an orthography someone declared. A layout that composes letters by rules may lack some, and one may type letters the language does not use (in Latin, every letter from a to z).* An inferred language's tooltip names every source it rests on and how much text each counted (*Unilex word frequencies (a word list of 23,512 letters) and Google Fonts' sample text (a text of 1,204 letters)*), so a designer can weigh it; one that rests on a single text of fewer than 3,000 letters says so: *It rests on one short text: the weakest evidence in this list.*

#### High-value languages

*High-value only* keeps the languages worth covering first: 300 orthographies of 284 languages. The rule is reproducible and its data openly licensed: *checks/train/high_value.py* makes *checks/high_value_languages.json* from Unicode CLDR 49 (Unicode License V3), the archive *orthographies.py* already pins. A language is of high value when at least one holds:

- **CLDR targets it at its *modern* coverage level** (*common/properties/coverageLevels.txt*, 100 locales): the languages the major platforms ship their software in. It stands for presence on the web and in software, which no openly licensed count measures: W3Techs' content-language shares and Ethnologue's speaker counts are proprietary, so they are cited, never used. The 40 languages most used for web content (English, Spanish, German, Japanese, French, Russian, Portuguese, and on to Bengali and Malay) are all in CLDR's modern set.
- **It is official, or official in fact, in a country or territory** (CLDR's territory data, `officialStatus` *official* or *de_facto_official*; a regional or minority status does not count): governments, schools, and signs set it.
- **At least 1,000,000 people read and write it**: each territory's population, times the share that uses the language (first and second language alike, as CLDR estimates it), times the share of those who write it (the language's own writing share where CLDR gives one, else its own literacy, else the territory's), summed over the territories. Fonts are read, so written use counts, not speech alone.

A CLDR code becomes Font Check's ISO 639-3 codes through CLDR's aliases: the three-letter form of a two-letter code, and the individual languages of a macrolanguage (Standard Arabic, *arb*, for *ar*; Mandarin, *cmn*, for *zh*), which Hyperglot lists. An orthography is of high value only in a script CLDR writes the language in (the script named in a locale's code, *sr_Latn*, else the language's primary scripts, else its likely one): Serbian in Cyrillic and Latin, Japanese in kanji, hiragana, and katakana, Russian in Cyrillic only. Each row of the file gives its reasons (*modern*, *official* and the territories, *writers* and their number). A search finds the languages left out under *Other languages*, whatever their status. Without the file, the choice keeps no language and the log says so; Glyph Compose, which asks Font Check for its high-value glyphs with Font Check's settings, follows the choice too.

### Opening

The window opens on a line that says what is being done (*Reading the language data…*, *Reading the font…*, *Checking 2,512 languages…*, *Listing…*), beside the progress bar ([below](#the-progress-bar-and-the-tooltips-made-when-asked-for)), and only there: the lists' area is blank meanwhile, the settings and the tabs grayed, and no caption counts anything before the check has run (*Show the scripts whose glyphs are fully missing from the font*, *High-value missing glyphs*). The check runs in four steps, each started from a zero timer once the window is shown, so Qt repaints between two; no step processes events, so the tool is never entered again from inside itself, and a check asked for meanwhile does not start. Every later check (the *Check* button, another choice of *Display*, an undo) shows the same. The top line is hidden meanwhile: emptied, it would still take a line over the progress bar.

Where the seconds go was measured with the tests' fakes under Python 2.7 (no Qt work is measured that way): reading the four data files, 45 ms; checking 2,549 orthographies and 61 sets, 170 ms; making the rows' data, 95 to 140 ms. So the Python side takes about a third of a second, and the rest is Qt's: some 2,600 rows made with a rich tooltip each, the columns sized to their contents, and the 8 MB list font shaped for the first paint. On the Python side, the data files are kept for FontLab's session (FontLab runs the script anew at each opening, so a module's own cache would last one window): a second opening reads nothing, 1 ms against 45. A tooltip's width is measured on its longest line first and no further once one is too wide (2,610 lines measured at an opening rather than 10,701), with one font metrics for all of them. The list font is registered once per session, and the rows of closed sections are not made.

#### The progress bar, and the tooltips made when asked for

A progress bar shows in the window's top row (FreeLab's shared one, [WINDOWS.md](WINDOWS.md)) while the window loads and while a check runs, with the step's name beside it (the one place it is said), and goes when the lists are shown. A check stopped meanwhile leaves the view as if it had not been checked: *Check*, or showing the view again, checks it. It is determinate: a tenth of its length when the data is read, a fifth when the font is, two fifths when the languages, sets, and scripts are checked, and the rest over the listing, by the rows made (the scripts' list, the list at the right section by section, the glyphs to add). Its chunk is the log's *done* green (the lighter of the pair on a dark dialog, the darker on a light one), set by the bar's own stylesheet, never the dialog's, and the bar is marked for dark mode to leave alone, so that the green is not remapped. It stands above the stack of the two pages, not on the blank page shown meanwhile: the lists' page is shown before it is filled (its columns are sized to it), and a bar on the other page would stop being painted then. The window repaints at each move, its events held back but for the controls that stop the work. The log gets one line when the check is done, not one per step: *Checked 2,512 languages, 140 character sets, and 172 scripts in 0.4 s.* (the five *Legacy* sets are not counted, [below](#optional-characters)).

Where the seconds go on Qt's side cannot be measured without FontLab. Measuring a line that names a language's missing characters in the dialog's font sends Qt looking for a font for each script named, which, for every row of the lists at the right, would be 2,600 times at an opening, for tooltips of which a handful are ever read. So a row's tooltip is made when Qt asks for it: the lists' event filter (the one that hears the presses) takes the *ToolTip* event, finds the row under the mouse, makes its text, and shows it over that row; the headings and the glyphs' lists, a few hundred rows at most, have theirs made with the list. The columns are sized once per filling, and only the tab shown is filled (the two others when first shown). What remains is Qt's own: about 2,600 rows made, the last column sized to its contents by the header, and the first paint in the 8 MB list font. To time in FontLab: the opening with the log's line, and whether the bar moves smoothly through the listing or sits at two fifths for most of it (the listing's share of the bar, `BUSY_SHARES`, is a guess until then).

### Character sets that are no languages

A font is also judged by sets no language defines: the IPA (a font without *ə* covers many languages and no phonetic transcription), mathematics, symbols. Font Check lists 145 of them in the *Character sets* tab, in nine sections (*Phonetics*, *Mathematics*, *Symbols and punctuation*, *CJK symbols and forms*, *Han repertoires*, *Notation*, *Games and pictographs*, *Legacy*, *Foundries*), checked as a language is: every character of a set is a base character but its optional ones ([below](#optional-characters)), so a set is covered when the font has them all, within reach when it misses at most the number set, started when it has at least one, and missed when it has none. A row says *158 of 168 missing*; its tooltip names the definition the set is cited by, the count the font has, and the first 60 characters missing. Selecting a set hands its missing characters to the second tab of characters as a language's are, to check, copy, or build with Glyph Compose. The sets take no part in the scripts' counts or in the languages gained; the top line counts those covered, but the *Legacy* sets. Each set is one of four kinds, and nothing else is called covered:

- **Unicode 17.0.0**: the assigned characters of 88 blocks (IPA Extensions, Phonetic Extensions and its Supplement, Spacing Modifier Letters, Modifier Tone Letters; Mathematical Operators and its Supplemental block, Miscellaneous Mathematical Symbols-A and -B, Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols, Letterlike Symbols, Number Forms, Superscripts and Subscripts, Arrows, Supplemental Arrows-A and -B, Miscellaneous Technical; General Punctuation, Currency Symbols, Box Drawing, Block Elements, Geometric Shapes, Miscellaneous Symbols, Dingbats; the 38 added after the survey, the 21 after the second one, and the five of *Legacy*, [below](#unicode-170-and-what-font-check-measures)), the 2,318 characters with the Math property (the set [Unicode Technical Report #25](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr25/) defines), and the currency symbols of any block (general category Sc). Control and format characters are left out: no glyph is drawn for them.
- **The Han repertoires**, 20 sets from the Unicode Character Database's Unihan fields (Unicode License V3), under *Han repertoires*: Unihan Core 2020 (20,720 ideographs, the largest); for Chinese, GB/T 2312-1980, the *Tōngyòng Guīfàn Hànzìbiǎo* of 2013, Big Five, and HKSCS-2008 and 2016; for Japanese, the *Jōyō* and *Jinmei-yō* kanji, JIS X 0208 and JIS X 0213's levels 3 and 4, and the kokuji; for Korean, KS X 1001's hanja, the basic education hanja, and the personal-name hanja; Sawndip; and for Vietnamese, TCVN 5773 and 6056, VHN 01 and 02, the Hán Nôm Coded Character Repertoire, and the Vietnamese extensions. They hold some 90,000 ideographs together, and each one of 1,000 characters or more is kept as ranges and checked on them, as the scripts are (`RangedSet`): opening Font Check makes no list of their characters, and selecting Unihan Core 2020 takes 23 ms, as Han's own selection does. The compatibility ideographs a repertoire holds are its optional characters.
- **Google Fonts' glyph sets** ([googlefonts/glyphsets](https://github.com/googlefonts/glyphsets) 1.1.3, Apache License 2.0): 25 sets, the phonetic ones (IPA Standard, IPA Historical, Disordered Speech, APA, SinoExt; Google calls them a work in progress) and the Latin, transliteration, Cyrillic, Greek, and Arabic ones. Only a set's encoded characters are checked, not the glyphs without a code point it also asks for, and each set is listed as Google defines it (*GF Latin Plus* holds what it adds to *GF Latin Core*, not both).
- **Adobe's character sets** (adobe-type-tools' [Latin](https://github.com/adobe-type-tools/adobe-latin-charsets), [Cyrillic](https://github.com/adobe-type-tools/adobe-cyrillic-charsets), and [Greek](https://github.com/adobe-type-tools/adobe-greek-charsets) lists): Adobe Latin 1 to 5, Cyrillic 1 to 3, and Greek 1 and 2, each including the smaller ones, with their letter-and-mark sequences Unicode has no single character for (counted when the font's anchors join them, as a language's are). Each repository carries the MIT License (Copyright Adobe Systems Incorporated), which allows the copy; Adobe calls the sets informative, not normative.

#### Optional characters

Some characters a set or a script holds are ones text does not store: presentation forms, which a font draws by shaping from the plain letters (Arabic's initial, medial, and final forms, Hebrew's letters with points, the *fi* and *fl* ligatures), and compatibility characters that normalization replaces by another (Han's compatibility ideographs, the Kelvin, ångström, and ohm signs). Rather than splitting the sets, the owner's design marks them **optional**: a set or a script is complete without them, and Font Check says how many the font lacks. *checks/character_sets.json* lists them (the scripts' rows' last column, `optional`, and the sets' in a top-level `optional` object by set id, which its `optional_about` describes): 728 of Arabic's 1,405 characters (its presentation forms; the 66 other characters of those blocks, signs in use, stay required), 45 of Hebrew's, 1,002 of Han's (its compatibility ideographs), 9 of Latin's, 5 of Armenian's, and one of Greek's (the ohm sign; its letters with oxia are required); three of *Letterlike Symbols*, two each of *Combining Diacritical Marks*, *Miscellaneous Technical*, and *General Punctuation*, one of the Math property's set, and the compatibility ideographs six Han repertoires hold (Unihan Core 2020, Big Five, HKSCS, the Jinmei-yō kanji, JIS X 0213, and KS X 1001). Google's and Adobe's sets have none.

- **They never decide a level.** A set is covered once it has every required character; within reach counts the required ones it misses; *started* asks for one of its required characters, so a font that has only the *fi* ligature has not started Latin. They take no part in the languages gained, the high-value glyphs, or the top line's count of sets covered, and break a tie last in the orders. They are never called auxiliary: a language's auxiliary character keeps it from being covered, an optional one does not.
- **They are counted and listed.** A row says *4 optional glyphs missing* when the set is covered otherwise, or *158 of 164 missing (and 4 optional glyphs)*, the noun following the number. Its tooltip counts them (*the font has 160 of its 164 characters, and 0 of the 4 optional ones*) and names them in a paragraph of their own, *Optional characters missing (presentation forms and compatibility characters, which text does not store)*, cut at 60 as the others are (by code point for a set the list's font does not draw). A script's definition says its presentation forms and compatibility characters are listed as optional. Selected, a set's own tab lists its required glyphs first and its optional ones after them, each marked *optional*, as many as the 200 rows leave room for; the line over it counts both (*Arabic misses 677 glyphs, and 728 optional glyphs, listed after them.*), and the tab's caption counts all it lists.
- **The Legacy group** holds the sets that are wholly optional: *Alphabetic Presentation Forms* (57), *Arabic Presentation Forms-A* (588) and *-B* (140), *CJK Compatibility Ideographs* (460) and its *Supplement* (542). Such a set is **always covered**: the choice made here, since text set in the font needs none of its characters, and a set colored red or gray for them would read as something to fix. They **do not count toward the stats** (the owner): the top line's count of sets covered and the log's count of sets checked leave them out (140 of the 145), since otherwise a Latin-only font would count as covering *CJK Compatibility Ideographs*; its row says how many the font lacks (*57 optional glyphs missing*), its tooltip *the font has 0 of its 57 characters, all optional: it is complete without them*, and selecting it lists them to build or copy. The group exists for these five only, just before *Foundries*.

Left out: the OpenType MATH table (it holds a font's constants, glyph variants, and assemblies for laying formulas out, and names no characters a font must have; whether a font has one is another check than coverage); WGL4 and W1G (no pinned list under a license checked here; the Adobe and Google Latin sets cover the same ground); Google's sets for the scripts Hyperglot's languages already describe one by one (Indic, Southeast Asian, and African scripts); and the other Unicode blocks, which a script's set or a language answers for ([the survey](#unicode-170-and-what-font-check-measures) says which).

*checks/character_sets.json* (427 KB, most of it the Han repertoires' ranges) is made by *checks/train/character_sets.py*, which downloads the sources at pinned versions outside the repository, checks each against its SHA-256, and writes each set's name, group, definition, and code points as ranges, then each script's name, its own characters, the characters it shares, and the version of Unicode it was first encoded in; the same sources give the same bytes. The scripts are in this file, not in a new one: they are sets, made from the same pinned database by the same script, and a file more would have to be installed, listed for the Python 2.7 gate, and kept in step. *checks/character_sets_LICENSE* carries the three notices (Unicode License V3, Apache License 2.0, MIT License). Without the file, the languages are listed alone.

```
python checks/train/character_sets.py --download <folder>
python checks/train/character_sets.py --download <folder> --survey
```

### Every script of Unicode, as a set

Every script Unicode encodes is a set of its own, 172 of them, whether or not an orthography is known, so that a font for a script no language here is written in can be measured: listed under *Scripts (Unicode)* in the *Character sets* tab, and, for the 60 without a language, as the live rows of *Scripts not yet covered* in the *Languages* tab (58 of them: Braille and SignWriting are notations, [above](#what-font-check-cannot-check-yet)). When a language is added for a script, the script leaves that section by itself and stays under *Scripts (Unicode)*.

- **The measure.** A script's **own** characters are those whose Script property is the script (*Scripts.txt*): its letters, marks, digits, and punctuation. They are the set's base characters. Characters of no one script (*Common*, *Inherited*) that Unicode assigns to the scripts that use them (*ScriptExtensions.txt*: the danda to Devanagari and twenty other scripts, the kana marks to Hiragana and Katakana), and the few characters of another script it is also written with, are its **shared** characters, 664 of them over all scripts: the set's auxiliary characters. So a script is *covered* when the font has both, *within reach* when it misses at most the number set of its own characters, or only shared ones (its row then says *3 shared missing*), *started*, or *missed*, as a language is. A *Common* or *Inherited* character without Script_Extensions (the digits 0 to 9, the comma, a space) is no script's, and is asked of no script: the Latin script's set holds no digit. Control and format characters are left out, as in every set (the Mongolian vowel separator: Mongolian counts 167). A letter the font's anchors compose counts, as in a language.
- **Large scripts.** Han has 103,351 characters of its own, Hangul 11,739, Tangut 7,059, Egyptian hieroglyphs 5,089, Latin 1,492, Arabic 1,405, Cuneiform 1,234, and Yi 1,220; the other 164 scripts have 726 at most, and together the 172 have 150,031. A script is kept as ranges and checked on them: no list of its characters is made, and its missing ones are code points that become characters when some are listed. Its row shows counts (*103,350 of 103,351 missing*), and selecting it lists the first 200 and counts the rest in the line over the list, as a large set does; the selection's tab looks at the first 2,000 missing of a set, never at all of them. Checking the 172 scripts takes 12 ms under Python 2.7 with the tests' fakes, and selecting Han 10 ms.
- **What the list font draws of them.** The bundled font cannot hold all of Unicode: a font has 65,535 glyphs at most, and this one used 37,267 for 29,402 characters. So the list of what it must draw (*checks/train/orthographies.py --characters*) is bounded by stated numbers: a script is drawn whole (its own and shared characters) up to **12,000** own characters (every script but Han, whose 103,351 ideographs pass a font's 65,535 glyphs), a block up to **1,100** (every block: the largest, Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols, has 996), and a Han repertoire up to **25,000** (every one: the largest, Unihan Core 2020, has 20,720), for 92,195 characters. Han alone is left out, of which FreeLang draws 35,525 ideographs (those of the repertoires and the orthographies). For a set left out, the pictures of the missing glyphs fall back to the system's fonts, and its tooltip names what is missing by code point (*U+2E80 to U+2E99, U+2E9B to U+2EF3, … and 102,009 more*, for a font with no ideograph), not by characters the tooltip's font would show as boxes; a set the font draws names the first 60 characters. The numbers stand in the train script (`SCRIPT_FONT_LIMIT`, `BLOCK_FONT_LIMIT`, `UNIHAN_FONT_LIMIT`) and in the tool (`NAMED_SCRIPT_MOST`, `NAMED_BLOCK_MOST`, `NAMED_UNIHAN_MOST`), where they decide only whether a tooltip names characters or code points (it makes its first 60 either way, so nothing is slower for them), and a test keeps them equal and checks that every character a set's tooltip names is in the list. Those bounds hold only with the optional files that draw most of it, *FreeLang CJK* and *FreeLang+ General*: `register_list_font` notes the fallback families it gave Qt (`LIST_FALLBACKS_DRAWN`), and a set past the first bounds whose characters a file that did not register draws (`fallbacks_needed`: a Han repertoire, a CJK script or block, the CJK file; Latin, Tangut, and Yi, which have some of its characters, both; any other, FreeLang+ General; Ethiopic and *Arabic Presentation Forms-A*, the first file alone) keeps the first bounds (a script of at most 300 own characters, a block of at most 256, and no Han repertoire), so its tooltip names code points rather than boxes. A test checks each case against what the build says each file holds.
- **The data.** *checks/train/character_sets.py* reads four more files of the Unicode Character Database 17.0.0, pinned by their SHA-256 as the others are: *Scripts.txt* (the file *orthographies.py* pins, at the same hash), *ScriptExtensions.txt*, *PropertyValueAliases.txt* (the scripts' names for the codes *ScriptExtensions.txt* uses), and *DerivedAge.txt* (the version each script was first encoded in, which the tooltip says: *Encoded since Unicode 16.0.*).

The owner: "We should also survey Unicode's exhaustive list of characters and see what's still missing, aside from languages." Unicode 17.0 assigns 159,866 code points to characters, the 65 control characters included (159,801 without them, the count Unicode states); private-use code points and surrogates are no characters and are not counted.

A character may stand in several columns. Before the scripts' sets, 130,533 characters were in no orthography and no set (most of them ideographs no orthography lists); with the scripts' sets, 5,766; with the first survey's 38 blocks, 943; with the second survey's 21, 499.

- **The 499 in none**, by block, the most first: Variation Selectors Supplement 240, Tags 97, Basic Latin 33 and Latin-1 Supplement 32 (their control characters), General Punctuation 21 (format characters), Egyptian Hieroglyph Format Controls 16, Variation Selectors 16, Arabic 8, Musical Symbols 8 (format characters), Tibetan 4, Specials 4, Shorthand Format Controls 4, and nine blocks with three or fewer (Latin Extended-D and -E, Devanagari, Kaithi, Mongolian, Syriac, and three Arabic blocks). They are characters no glyph is drawn for (230 control and format characters, 256 variation selectors), and 13 others (a letter, two marks, and ten symbols of scripts' blocks) that no orthography or set asks for.
- **Blocks no character set touches.** Of Unicode's 346 blocks, 211 have no character in any of the 145 sets. The characters of 199 of them are all in a script's set (the scripts' own blocks: the ideographs, Hangul, every historic script). The other 12 are the variation selectors, the tags, the format controls of Egyptian hieroglyphs and of shorthands, and Tibetan, Mongolian, Devanagari, Syriac, Kaithi, and two Arabic blocks, which lack a few *Common* characters each.
- **What was added: 38 blocks**, each a set named as the others are (*Musical Symbols (Unicode block)*), 6,063 characters, from the pinned *Blocks.txt*. They are whole blocks a type designer may set out to draw whose characters nothing answered for: in *Mathematics*, Supplemental Arrows-C; in *Symbols and punctuation*, Supplemental Punctuation, Miscellaneous Symbols and Arrows, Miscellaneous Symbols Supplement, Geometric Shapes Extended, Ornamental Dingbats, Enclosed Alphanumerics and its Supplement, Control Pictures, Optical Character Recognition, Symbols for Legacy Computing and its Supplement, Alchemical Symbols, Ancient Symbols, and Phaistos Disc; in *Notation*, a new section, Musical Symbols, Byzantine Musical Symbols, Znamenny Musical Notation, Ancient Greek Musical Notation, Braille Patterns, Sutton SignWriting, Duployan (the shorthands), Counting Rod Numerals, Mayan Numerals, Kaktovik Numerals, Indic and Ottoman Siyaq Numbers, Tai Xuan Jing Symbols, and Yijing Hexagram Symbols; in *Games and pictographs*, a new section, Chess Symbols, Playing Cards, Domino Tiles, Mahjong Tiles, Miscellaneous Symbols and Pictographs, Emoticons, Transport and Map Symbols, Supplemental Symbols and Pictographs, and Symbols and Pictographs Extended-A (the emoji blocks, as text: their monochrome glyphs). Braille, SignWriting, and Duployan are scripts too, and are also under *Scripts (Unicode)*; they are listed as blocks because that is how a designer names them.
- **What the second survey added: 21 blocks**, every block of *Blocks.txt* checked against the sets: those whose characters are no one script's own (*Common* or *Inherited*, which *ScriptExtensions.txt* may lend to several scripts) and that no set held whole. In *Phonetics*, the combining marks (Combining Diacritical Marks, its Extended and Supplement blocks, and Combining Half Marks), which orthographies ask for one by one; in *Mathematics*, Combining Diacritical Marks for Symbols and Arabic Mathematical Alphabetic Symbols (as its Latin counterpart, Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols); in *CJK symbols and forms*, a new section, CJK Symbols and Punctuation, Ideographic Description Characters, Kanbun, CJK Strokes, Enclosed CJK Letters and Months, CJK Compatibility, Enclosed Ideographic Supplement, Vertical Forms, CJK Compatibility Forms, Small Form Variants, and Halfwidth and Fullwidth Forms (the first survey had left the compatibility forms to a Chinese, Japanese, or Korean font's own collection, Adobe-Japan1 and the like; a font for those languages is measured by them all the same); in *Notation*, Aegean Numbers, Coptic Epact Numbers, Common Indic Number Forms, and Vedic Extensions, numbers and marks several scripts share.
- **And then the Legacy blocks: 5**, wholly optional ([above](#optional-characters)): Alphabetic Presentation Forms, Arabic Presentation Forms-A and -B, CJK Compatibility Ideographs and its Supplement, 1,787 characters. Text does not store them, so they decide nothing; a font for legacy data or for a system that still sends them may want them, and the rows say how many it lacks.
- **And the Han repertoires: 20 sets** ([above](#character-sets-that-are-no-languages)), which take 35,162 more ideographs into the sets (the column *In a character set*: 14,811 before them, and ten blocks the sets did not touch): they measure a Chinese, Japanese, Korean, or Vietnamese font by the repertoires its readers expect, where the Han script's 103,351 ideographs are no target.
- **What was not added**: private-use, surrogate, control, and unassigned ranges, which hold no characters; the variation selectors, the tags, the specials, and the format controls (Egyptian hieroglyphs', shorthands'), for which no glyph is drawn; and the blocks of one script's own characters, which its script's set answers for (their presentation forms and compatibility characters, where they have any, are that set's optional ones).

Glyph Compose takes the list: `main(characters=[...])` hands the letters with diacritics to its *Diacritics* tab, whose *Character set* box then reads *From Font Check (N glyphs)*, and the other characters of its Glyph generation table to that tab, whose *List* box reads the same (Font Check still falls back to `main()` for a Glyph Compose that takes no list).

### Why these parts

FontLab 7 and 8 have no view of language coverage (FontLab 8's audit covers kerning) and draw no metrics. Glyphs relies on plugins: TalkingLeaves lists the languages a font nearly supports from Hyperglot's data, with a button to add glyphs, and mekkablue's Vertical Metrics Manager reports the highest and lowest glyphs and writes the values; Hyperglot's web app lists the languages supported per script; Google Fonts' metrics guide is text only. None of them ranks the missing characters by the languages they would complete, and none draws the metrics: those two ideas carry this design.

### The data

*checks/language_coverage.json* is made by *checks/train/languages.py* from Hyperglot 0.8.2 (its release archive, downloaded over HTTPS and checked against its SHA-256): 829 orthographies of 799 languages, 260 KB. For each language it keeps the primary orthographies (and the historical ones of historical languages), as Hyperglot's own language detection does; secondary orthographies and transliterations are left out. Hyperglot's inheritance (`<iso>`, `<iso auxiliary>`, `<iso Script>`, `<default Script>`) is resolved, characters are kept composed (NFC), digraphs are split into their letters (Hyperglot does not check them as units either), and the marks kept are Hyperglot's "required" ones: listed marks no character of the orthography decomposes into, and the marks of letter-and-mark clusters Unicode has no single character for. Lists shared by several orthographies (Chinese's) are stored once. *checks/language_coverage_LICENSE* carries Hyperglot's license and says what was changed.

```
python checks/train/languages.py --download <folder>
```

#### The languages Hyperglot does not describe

*checks/language_orthographies.json* (400 KB) is made by *checks/train/orthographies.py*: 1,816 orthographies of 1,779 languages (1,713 that Hyperglot does not describe, and 66 of Hyperglot's in a script it does not give them in), in the shape of Hyperglot's file plus three columns: each row's source, its evidence, and, for an inferred row, every source it rests on with the letters that source counted. It also holds what is known of the scripts beyond its rows (Unicode's scripts no orthography is written in, and the scripts CLDR or Google's data records for languages no source gives characters for). It is a second file, read beside the first: Hyperglot's own file stays what *languages.py* alone makes of Hyperglot's release, and the two together are smaller than Glottolog's. Hyperglot is the first choice: a language it describes is taken from it alone, in the scripts it gives. A declared source may add a script Hyperglot does not give the language in (Javanese in Javanese, Kazakh in Arabic, English in Deseret and Shavian: 64 such rows of 62 languages), each script from the first declared source below that has it, under Hyperglot's name for the language; only a subtag that names the language itself counts, since a macrolanguage's (*uz*, *ff*, *sw*) does not say which of its members Hyperglot's is (Uzbek in Arabic is Southern Uzbek's, not Northern's). Nothing counted is added to a language Hyperglot describes, nor to one it describes through its members by Glottolog's classification: An Crúbadán's file of Akan makes no row, since Hyperglot describes Akan's dialects Twi and Fante (CLDR files Twi under *ak* and has no alias for *aka*). A language it lacks is taken from the first declared source below that has it, and from that one alone, with every script that source gives: a declared orthography is never mixed with anything. A language no declared source has is taken from the inferred sources, all of those that have it, put together as [reinforcement](#reinforcement) says. A keyboard layout ([below](#keyboard-layouts)) ranks between the two kinds and stands alone: it adds a language no source describes, or a script no source gives a language in (beside Hyperglot's or a declared source's rows, never mixed into them), and where counted sources give a language in its script, its row takes their place. Two declared rows are amended where the writing's history or the source's own text shows its exemplars wrong (`AMENDED`): English in Deseret takes as base the 38 letters every printed book set, in two cases (*Deseret First Book* and *Second Book*, 1868; the *Book of Mormon*, 1869; the *Deseret News*), and as auxiliary letters oi and ew (𐐦 𐐧 𐑎 𐑏), which the *Deseret News* approved in 1859 and never cast, and which SLDR lists with the others; a font of the printed alphabet is within reach, not started. English in Shavian takes the namer dot (·, U+00B7), which marks a proper name as a capital does in Latin, beside its 48 letters: SLDR's exemplars leave it out, while its own names of months and days use it. The auxiliary characters' tooltip line reads *(loan words, names, rare letters)*.

An orthography a person or a maintained project **declares**, one **inferred** from counting a text, and the characters a **keyboard** layout types are not the same evidence, and the data (*d*, *i*, and *k*), the list, and the tooltips keep them apart.

An inferred language is counted here under the first source it rests on; 211 of the 767 inferred orthographies rest on two or more (SLDR's generated exemplars take part in 501, Unilex in 324, the sample texts in 94, the trigrams in 52, the declaration in 16, the archives in 3). So 940 languages are added from declared orthographies, 748 from inferred ones, and 24 from keyboard layouts alone. A text's script is found by Unicode's own code (*PropertyValueAliases.txt*) where neither the train script's table nor Google's name for the script gives it: that adds Lisu in Lisu (Unilex) and Mandaic in Mandaic (a sample text), and reads Ho's sample text as Warang Citi, which Ho's layout replaces. The table's declared counts include the second scripts of Hyperglot's languages. Glottolog (CC BY 4.0, the source of the families) gives a language's English name where no source does, its constructed status, and which codes name a language; the license file says so. Each archive is downloaded over HTTPS outside the repository and checked against its SHA-256, read without being extracted, and the same sources give the same bytes. An Crúbadán's files come one at a time, a pause before each, into a folder of their own (*--texts*, the folder *crubadan* beside the first by default), and so do Language Tools' layout files (*--keyboards*, the folder *keyboards*). *checks/language_orthographies_LICENSE* carries the notices and says what was changed.

What the sources give, and what is made of it:

- **Base, auxiliary, marks.** Google's data gives the three, as Hyperglot does, and is read the same way (the marks kept are the listed marks no character decomposes into, and those of letter-and-mark clusters with no single character). CLDR and SLDR give a main and an auxiliary set and no list of marks: a lone combining mark in the main set is a mark the orthography needs (the vowel signs of an Indic script), one in the auxiliary set is left out (Hyperglot's auxiliary characters hold none either). A counted text gives **one set only**, taken as base characters: no auxiliary set is made up.
- **Capitals.** CLDR's and SLDR's exemplars list small letters only (UTS #35); each one's capital is added, since Hyperglot's and Google's lists hold both and a font is checked for both. Georgian's Mtavruli capitals are not added.
- **Left out.** Format characters (the joiners of some exemplar sets) and spaces: no glyph is asked for them. SLDR's alternates (`alt`), which are proposals, among them its raw counts marked *suspect* ("data that is considered wrong"). Regional and variant locales: a language's own file and its scripts' are read.
- **The rule of a counted text** (Unilex, An Crúbadán, Google's sample texts), stated so that nothing is filtered by taste: the text in NFC; only letters count (general category L) with the combining marks after them, never digits, punctuation, or symbols; the script is the one most of the letters are in (Unicode 17.0.0's *Scripts.txt*), and letters of another script are dropped (quotations, foreign names) while letters of no script of their own are kept (the modifier apostrophe); capitals are counted with their small letters; a character is kept when it makes at least **1 letter in 2,000** and stands in at least **3 word forms** (Unilex, An Crúbadán's archives) or 3 places (a sample text); a text of fewer than **1,000 letters** gives nothing. In Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, and Armenian text a letter is counted with the marks on it (Yoruba's *ẹ́*); elsewhere letters and marks are counted one by one. SLDR's generated exemplars are taken as SLDR gives them: its tools' rule is SIL's, not this one.
- **Trigrams.** An Crúbadán's 2010 files hold no words: each lists every run of three characters of every word, the word set between *<* and *>*, with its count, down to the runs seen once (426 of the 449 files; none is cut to its commonest). Every character of a text is the middle one of exactly one such run, so the middle characters are counted: that gives each letter's exact count, and the two markers, which are no letters, never count. A letter is taken with the combining mark after it (one mark: a run shows no further). The word forms cannot be counted, so a letter must be the middle of **3 different trigrams** instead (three different pairs of neighbors), and the 1,000 letters are those counted.
- **A script stated.** An Crúbadán states no script: the one counted is taken. Where Google's data states the language's scripts (in any of its language files, whether or not its text counts: the filter was built from the texts that did) and the one counted is another (Gothic, crawled in Latin letters), the language is left to Google's sample text; where they agree, the name, autonym, and status Google's data gives the language are kept (Old English stays historical).
- **Codes.** A BCP 47 language subtag's ISO 639-3 code comes from CLDR's alias table (the three-letter code of a two-letter subtag, and the member languages a macrolanguage's subtag stands for): of those, the first Glottolog has. Ten subtags name no language Glottolog knows and are left out (the groups *art*, *ber*, *gem*, *sla*, and *mis*; the private-use *qaz*; and *adb*, *ffi*, *kze*, *pxm*, which Glottolog keeps among its retired entries). An Crúbadán's 2010 table gives ISO 639-3 codes: each is read as the subtag CLDR writes it with (Plateau Malagasy, *plt*, as Malagasy, which Hyperglot has). Nine of its codes were retired since and are left out too: *fri*, *mly*, *gsc*, *lms*, *lnc*, and *prv* (Western Frisian, Malay, and Occitan, which Hyperglot describes under their present codes), *btb*, *ccx*, and *tzc*. The declaration's files are named the same way, and five more of their codes were retired since (*auv*, *blu*, *flm*, *hsf*, *hva*).
- **Files under another language's code.** Two entries of the 2010 table are doubtful: *aja* ("Aja", which ISO 639-3 gives to Aja of South Sudan, a Kresh-Aja language) and *hna* ("Mina", ISO 639-3's Mina of Cameroon, a Chadic language). Each trigram file was compared with every text of the declaration by the cosine of their trigram counts (1 for the same text). The *aja* file is the declaration in Aja of Benin (*ajg*, a Gbe language: 0.99 with that text, 0.41 with Ewe's and Fon's; open o and e, African d, eng). The *hna* file is the declaration in Gen (*gej*, the Gbe language of Togo and Benin also called Mina, a name Glottolog lists for it): 0.65 with the Aja of Benin text and 0.50 with Ewe's, its nearest, and the *UDHR in Unicode* project of the time filed the same text under *hna* too (1.00). The test found a third the same way, *ako* ("Akurio", a Cariban language of Suriname): the declaration in Waama (*wwa*, a Gur language of Benin, 1.00), and a fourth of another kind, *tos* (Highland Totonac), the text the declaration's project files under *top* (Papantla Totonac, 0.95): one text under two labels, kept once, under the code of the project that published it. The source's label says one thing and its content another, and the content is trusted, since it can be checked against texts whose codes ISO 639-3's registry and Glottolog confirm. Each file is read as the language its text is in (the script's `MISFILED` table, with the evidence): Hyperglot describes Aja of Benin, Gen, and Waama, so nothing is added under any of those codes. No minimum of text would have caught them (the files count 32,641, 16,672, and 6,786 letters; the smallest file kept counts 3,666, and a text under 1,000 letters gives nothing already), so the rule is the test itself: the build reports any trigram file whose nearest text of the declaration, at 0.9 or more, is another code's. Of the 273 files whose code has a text there, the nearest is their own for 256; the others are close relatives (Zulu and Ndebele, Bosnian and Croatian) or another script.

Unicode stopped hosting the project in January 2024 and points to the United Nations' site, which has no release to pin: hence NLTK's copy of 2012.

#### Reinforcement

No inferred language is dropped, however short its text. Where two or more inferred sources describe a language in the same script, their evidence is put together, and three ways of doing so were measured against Hyperglot on the orthographies it shares, with the script's own functions:

| On 220 orthographies with two or more independent counted sources | Base characters lacking (median, mean) | Beyond base and auxiliary (median, mean) | Has all of Hyperglot's base |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The first source alone | 8, 10.2 | 0, 3.7 | 25 |
| The union of what each source's rule keeps | 6, 8.8 | 0, 4.6 | 31 |
| **A letter one source's rule keeps and another source has too, or one that makes 1 letter in 500 alone** | 7, 9.6 | 0, 3.7 | 28 |

The union gains the most letters and takes every source's foreign ones with them. Two more were tried on an earlier grouping: the union with a higher floor (2, 4, or 10 times) for letters only one source keeps, which lacked more than the first source alone (8 against 7), since it also drops the first source's own rare letters; and pooling the raw counts and applying the rule once, which did as the union where the counts are true counts (6 lacking, 1 beyond) and needs Unilex left aside (its counts are per billion words). The rule taken is the third: it lacks one letter fewer at the median and no more beyond, because a letter two texts share is rarely foreign and a letter one crawl alone picked up often is. With SLDR's generated exemplars among the sources (50 orthographies), which give no counts and many letters, it lacks 3 and has 12 beyond, against 3.5 and 15 for SLDR's set alone: a letter of SLDR's goes when the counted sources beside it never saw it, provided they counted 5,000 letters or more between them (a sample text of 1,200 letters took 13 of Syloti Nagri's letters otherwise).

Evidence is combined only when it is independent. Google's sample text is an excerpt of the declaration, so it adds nothing where the whole text is there, and the whole text takes its place. Many of An Crúbadán's smaller corpora are that one document: of 301 trigram files with a text of their code in the declaration, 86 have a cosine of 0.9 or more with it and 82 more of 0.8 to 0.9. A trigram file at 0.8 or more counts as the same text, and gives way to the text itself (14 orthographies).

Of the orthographies a sample text gives:

- 5 (Matu Chin, Mozarabic, Unified Quichua, Arequipa-La Unión Quechua, Güilá Zapotec) rest on the declaration's whole text, 7,025 to 10,377 letters instead of the excerpt's 2,054 at most, and gain 2 to 12 base characters by it (*F Q f q À È Ò à è ò* for Mozarabic);
- 77 rest on a sample text alone, of 1,000 to 2,054 letters, and their rows say *It rests on one short text*: 29 are historical languages in their own scripts (Ugaritic, Lycian, Old Uyghur, Gothic), which no other counted source has, and the declaration does not exist in them; three are a script Google's data states for a language whose other script another source gives (Newa for Newari, Kirat Rai for Bantawa, Arabic for Maranao); one is Mandaic in Mandaic (its script found by Unicode's own code);
- their measured quality is the sample texts' (a median of 24 base characters lacking, 14 without Chinese, Japanese, and Korean; 18 and 14.5 by the second harness), and that of the five is the whole texts' (8).

211 orthographies rest on two or more sources (168 on SLDR's generated exemplars and Unilex; Sylheti's in Syloti Nagri among them: its layouts are not read, [below](#keyboard-layouts)). The 5,000-letter condition leaves at most 8 of them as SLDR gives them.

#### Keyboard layouts

[Language Tools](https://languagetools-153419.appspot.com/), which Craig Cornelius maintains with Translation Commons, serves keyboard layouts for some 90 languages, many of them written in scripts Unicode encoded recently. Its source, [sven-oly/LanguageTools](https://github.com/sven-oly/LanguageTools), carries the Apache License 2.0 (its *LICENSE* file), and most layout files open with the license's header; 20 of the 131 read carry no license line of their own (a "Copyright 2017 Google Inc. All Rights Reserved." line on eight prototypes, Bassa Vah's, Chakma's, Rohingya's, and one of Mende's, and "All Rights Reserved." alone on Rohingya's Arabic one; the names of its authors on one Oji-Cree layout; nothing on the others), and the repository's *LICENSE* is what covers them, which the license file says. Two rows rest on such files: Bassa in Bassa Vah and Severn Ojibwa. What the data keeps of a layout is the list of characters it types, a fact; no key map or rule is kept. Its fonts, most of which carry no license, are not used. The live branch is *p3_version* (commits to 2026-10-23); *master* stopped on 2023-12-21. Its *static/layouts* folder holds every layout of *master*'s but one (James Bay Cree's, under an older name) and 25 more, and ten files changed since, so the data is pinned at the head of *p3_version*, commit `a9386e0031b086418f402338a7428449b08166a1`, read on 2026-11-13; each file is fetched alone from *raw.githubusercontent.com* and checked against its SHA-256 (the repository is 187 MB of fonts).

A keyboard layout is evidence of a third kind, *keyboard* (*k* in the data): neither an orthography a maintained source declares nor a text counted, but a keyboard author's statement of the characters a language's writers type.

- **The mapping.** A layout's file name is no reliable code (Gurung Khema's file is there as *gvr* and as *grv*, Grebo's code; *tcy*, Tulu's, types Malayalam). The train script's `KEYBOARDS` table maps each layout used to an ISO 639-3 code and an ISO 15924 script by hand, from its title and what it types, with a comment where the mapping is not obvious: Gunjala Gondi is Adilabad Gondi's and Masaram Gondi Aheri Gondi's (CLDR's likely subtags), Gondi in Devanagari is Northern Gondi (CLDR's member of the macrolanguage), the Cham script Eastern Cham's (the file is named *cja*, Western Cham), Lenape Unami, the Adlam layout Pular's, the Yezidi one Northern Kurdish's, Tamashek's Tawallammat Tamajaq's (CLDR's member of *tmh*), and the Oji-Cree layouts Severn Ojibwa's. Not read: the layouts of scripts Unicode has not encoded (Bete, Kpelle, Loma, Qiang's Rma, Kurmali's Chisoi, Lampung, Wolof's Catya, Nsibidi), Kaktovik numerals, a family's (Oto-Manguean), English, emoji, legacy-font encodings in Latin letters, a proposed new spelling of Vietnamese, Irish in Ogham, and the layouts whose language their name does not settle ("Fort Severn Cree", "Cree Central 1", "James Bay Cree", "Anangu Yolngu"), and Sylheti's two in Syloti Nagri, which have no key for *kho*, *cho*, or the anusvara, so the row Sylheti's counted sources make is the better one; Yoruba's of 1983 and the Swiss French one are left out by name, being another orthography's and another country's.
- **The reading rule.** Every layer's keys and every rule's replacement (the transform that turns what is typed into letters: a phonetic layout composes most of its letters so) are read: the JavaScript strings decoded (a `\uXXXX` pair of surrogates is one character), the `{{ }}` groups of a key taken whole (a label set apart: `{{S||label||text}}`), each in NFC. Kept are the letters of the row's script and modifier letters of no script of their own (the modifier apostrophe; not a micro sign a Tifinagh layout types), and the combining marks of the script or shared with it (*Scripts.txt*, *ScriptExtensions.txt*); in Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, and Armenian, a letter with the marks on it, as a counted text's, and the general diacritics typed alone. Digits, punctuation, private-use, and unassigned code points are dropped: the general categories are the pinned *UnicodeData.txt*'s, since Python's own data is older (the Tai Yo layouts type 11 code points Unicode 17.0 leaves unassigned, nine of them in the block). For a script other than Latin, a layer all of whose letters are Latin is the keyboard's Latin fallback (the layers of Control or caps lock, often) and is not read. **For Latin, a to z and their capitals are taken with what the layout adds**: the layout types them, and so the beyond of a Latin layout counts the letters of a to z its language does not use. Capitals are added as for CLDR's exemplars; a layout gives one set, taken as base characters, its lone marks as marks.
- **The floor.** A layout must type at least 20 letters and marks of its script (in Latin, a to z count), of which at least 2 an English keyboard does not type. It is chosen from the data: the layouts named for a script they do not type give none of its characters (Mende Kikakui's three, Tulu's, Wolof's in Garay, Zaghawa's in Beria Erfe), and the English layout under another name gives nothing beyond a to z (White Hmong's, Singpho's, and Lakota's plain one); the fewest kept are 25 outside Latin (Batak, Sora Sompeng) and 2 beyond a to z in Latin (Menominee's first). Between them falls only a five-letter Assamese prototype. Ten layouts fall under it. Of a language and script's several layouts, the one that types the most is read (ties: the first in the table).
- **Unfinished layouts.** A layout whose layers (but its Latin fallback) or rules type more than 2 letters of a script other than its own, Latin, and Common or Inherited is refused: it is a prototype copied from another script's layout. Only letters count (general category L), a small letter and its capital once. Gondi in Telugu (*gon_telu*) types 6 Tibetan letters, with Tibetan vowel signs, on its shift layer, plain Latin on its caps-lock layers, and has one placeholder rule; in place of the counted row it would lack 5 independent vowels and 8 of its 11 marks, so SLDR's generated exemplars stay. The 2 allowed are measured: finished layouts type a stray one or two (the three Ahom star layouts a Sogdian letter, refused they would leave Ahom to a layout typing 8 fewer; a Catio layout a Hangul vowel; Sunuwar's an omega; Ho-Chunk's a theta and a chi on its Control layers), and of the 131 read, none but Gondi in Telugu and two under the floor (Mende Kikakui's, 28 Bassa Vah letters; Tulu's, 33 Malayalam) types more.
- **The rank, and no reinforcement.** Measured against Hyperglot as the inferred sources are (the table above), a layout lacks a median of 2 base characters and has 3 beyond, against 3 and 10 for SLDR's generated exemplars, the best of the inferred; the mean lacking (11.5) is the worst, for phonetic layouts that compose letters by rules and for prototypes (Inuktitut's lacks 68, Mon's 60, Yoruba's 30 of its accented vowels, which it types as marks). So it ranks after every declared source and before every inferred one. Whether a layout may be a second witness for a counted text was measured on the 18 orthographies Hyperglot shares with a layout and counted sources: the counted row lacks a median of 11.5 (mean 15.2) and has 0 beyond (mean 2.2); the layout alone, 1.5 (12.0) and 2 (7.3); the two put together by the reinforcement rule, the layout as a source without counts, 3 (9.8) and 0.5 (3.8). Against the counted row the excess rises, and against the layout, which ranks first, the lacking does: the rule asks of a second source that it lower the one without raising the other, so a layout is never a second witness. Its row rests on it alone, and takes the place of a counted row of the same language and script (seven: Plains Cree, Emberá-Catío, Khamti, S'gaw Karen, Lushootseed, Mru, and Mundari in Nag Mundari), keeping what the counted row's source said of the language (its name and status).
- **No count of what a layout lacks.** Before a layout takes a counted row's place, nothing is asked of the letters it lacks, because no count of them tells a worse layout from a better one. Plains Cree's lacks the most of its counted row (24 of 110 letters, against 75 added: the eastern finals, Th-Cree's and Sayisi's letters, and the w-dots of other Cree that the crawl mixed in), and it is the best of the seven; Emberá-Catío's lacks 10 vowels with an acute or a tilde, which its rules type as a mark after the vowel (it has the marks); the others lack 0 to 6 letters and marks against 7 to 17 added. The one clearly worse, Sylheti's in Syloti Nagri (5 lacking, 1 added: its *kho*, *cho*, and anusvara are on no key of either layout; the modifier apostrophe and a poetry mark were the counted row's noise), is left out of the table by hand, and its counted row stays. Lushootseed's lacks the *c*, *s*, and *x* with a caron (a dead caron key its stub transform gives no rule), and its counted row the *l* with a stroke, *p*, and *q*: neither is the better.

Not taken.
- **Wikipedia.** Of 348 open editions, 69 are in a language Hyperglot lacks, and about 30 in a language none of the sources above gives either. A dump per edition has no release to pin together, and a wiki's text is full of other languages; the letters a text uses are facts, which CC BY-SA does not reach, but the gain is too small for a download of that size. Wikipedia is not used for the families either: Glottolog classifies every language added.

*checks/language_families.json* (937 KB) is made by *checks/train/families.py* from Glottolog 5.4 (Hammarström, Forkel, Haspelmath, and Bank, 2026; Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International), as *glottolog/glottolog-cldf* publishes it: four files at the release's commit, downloaded over HTTPS outside the repository and each checked against its SHA-256. For each ISO 639-3 code of the two orthography files it keeps the names of the family and branches above the language (a table of 3,209 nodes, 77 of them families none of whose languages is described), its other names but those its orthographies already give it, and, for the 5,035 other languages with a code (not those described through a dialect or member: Latvian through Standard Latvian, Akan through Twi and Fante), their name, their family (its top node: their branches are not kept), and other names (without the two largest lists Glottolog compiled, MultiTree's and Lexvo's, which mostly repeat the others: with them the file would pass 1 MB). A code Glottolog gives to a dialect hangs under its language (Croatian, Bosnian, Serbian, and Montenegrin under Serbian-Croatian-Bosnian); fourteen codes Glottolog has under no languoid, ISO macrolanguages mostly (Balochi, Bikol, Kanuri, Kongo, Malagasy, Ojibwa, Oriya, Quechua, Sardinian), two Hyperglot files otherwise (*xha* for Minaean, *lab* for Linear A), two of the other sources' (*nwx*, Middle Newar, beside Kathmandu Valley Newari; *xmn*, Manichaean Middle Persian, beside Pahlavi), and Minang (*xrg*), are placed by hand in the script's `PLACED` table. Glottolog 5.4's *languages.csv* has no languoid with Minang's ISO 639-3 code (its *Minang* is a MultiTree name of Minangkabau, and *Mirning* is another family). Hyperglot's *Minang (Mirnong)* is a Noongar variety of the south-west of Western Australia, and it hangs beside Glottolog's Nyunga (*nyun1247*, ISO *nys*), under Pama-Nyungan. So every language checked is classified, and the *Languages Glottolog does not classify* node is not made; it stays for a code Glottolog does not have. Every language added sits in the *Families* tab. *checks/language_families_LICENSE* carries Glottolog's citation, what was changed, and the license. Without the file, the languages are listed without families (all under *Languages Glottolog does not classify*) and found by their own names only.

```
python checks/train/families.py --download <folder>
```

For the families and the other names, Glottolog was chosen over the two other sources looked at. Hyperglot itself holds no classification and no list of other names (33 languages have a preferred name beside their ISO name, which the data already uses). The ISO 639-3 name index (SIL) may be put in a product only if the product "does not provide a means to redistribute the code set", and its site is "the only authorized distribution site", which a GPL repository cannot promise. Wikidata's labels and aliases are CC0, but have no release to pin: a query gives another answer each day. Glottolog's release is pinned by a commit, keyed by ISO 639-3 code, and gives the classification and the names from one source. Its names come from its own records and from lists it compiled (MultiTree, Lexvo, the Catalogue of Endangered Languages, WALS, AIATSIS, Moseley & Asher 1994, Ruhlen 1987); Glottolog publishes the whole under CC BY 4.0, and the script's provider lists say which of them the file takes.

### The languages list's font

The lists of languages, character sets, and families are drawn in one font of their own, *FreeLang* (five files, 31.1 MB together, described below, all kept in *checks/language_font*), at 1.25 times the dialog's size, their rows, section headings, and column headings alike. The tooltips that name languages or characters are set in it too, at Qt's own size, and the pictures of the missing characters are drawn in it. In the dialog's font, Qt 5 takes each character the font lacks from another one: rare scripts' autonyms and characters (Canadian syllabics, Tifinagh, Vai, Tai Viet, Tai Tham, Ethiopic, and others) show as boxes, since Qt searches only some of the installed fonts, and some Cyrillic autonyms are spaced wide. The bundled font has every character the lists, tooltips, and pictures show: the languages' names and autonyms in every script, the script, group, family, and set names, the languages' other names, the digits and punctuation marks, the characters of every orthography's base, auxiliary, and mark lists, which the *Missing* column shows (a mark on a dotted circle), and the character sets' characters. The font claims each writing system it draws (OS/2 Unicode ranges and code pages), or Qt 5 would take another family for that script's text. Its line holds the deepest and highest ink of the names, autonyms, and headings (Tibetan stacks, a capital with two accents), each run of one script shaped by itself, as Qt shapes it: 1.143 em above the baseline and 0.472 em below, so rows are a little taller than the dialog's text. Of the orthographies' characters, 49 reach past that line (tall cuneiform signs for the most part, the tallest 0.45 em higher, the deepest 0.22 em lower). Without the file, the list keeps the dialog's font, still enlarged, and the tooltips and pictures keep theirs.

A character goes where its source font goes: three shared characters are therefore in the FreeLang+ General font, as each already came from a script's font (the combining dot above, U+0307, from Noto Sans Coptic; U+0358 from Noto Sans Osage; U+204F from Noto Sans Adlam). No font repeats a character of another: a script font's dotted circle, joiners, and digits are Noto Sans's.

Font Check registers the five with Qt (`register_list_font`), each once a session, and gives Qt the four others as substitutes of the first family (`QFont.insertSubstitutions`), so every use of the one family, *FreeLang Essential*, draws what it lacks from *FreeLang CJK*, then from *FreeLang+ General*, *FreeLang Mongolian–Tibetan*, and *FreeLang+ Duployan*. A fallback file that is missing or that Qt refuses leaves its characters to the system's fonts. That is what makes the four fallback fonts optional. The repository and each release hold all five since 0.3.0 (the repository kept the Essential font alone before, 2.0 MB of the 31.1, and the four others came in an archive of their own beside a release: a copy without them surprised with boxes, the owner, 2026-11-24). Without them, Windows' own fonts draw CJK and most other scripts in another design, and some characters as boxes; without the Duployan font alone, nothing changes but the Duployan character set's 143 characters, which no language's row shows. The installer skips a file a copy of the repository lacks, and the tests pass with or without them. The five are on 1,000 units per em and share one line, measured once over what all five draw (1.143 em above the baseline, 0.472 em below, no line gap), so a row that mixes them sits on one line. A tool that names the font to the user says *FreeLang* (`LIST_DISPLAY_NAME`), and every family's name starts with *FreeLang* and a space, which is how Glyph Compose leaves the five out of its font chooser.

A source keeps its layout (`GSUB`, `GPOS`) only if some character it supplies is in a language's name, autonym, other name, or orthography, since only those are ever set as running text; a source whose characters are a character set's alone, shown one at a time in a list cell, a tooltip, or a picture, keeps its character map alone. By that rule 103 subsets keep their layout (22 in Essential, 3 in CJK, 77 in FreeLang+ General, and Noto Serif Tibetan) and 71 do not (3 in Essential and 68 in FreeLang+ General: the fonts that never did, such as the mathematical and symbol fonts, and those whose scripts no language's row is written in). A declared orthography in a second script counts too (Javanese, Balinese, Sundanese, Nandinagari, Deseret, Shavian, and the Ajami rows), so those scripts' fonts keep their layout. Without the rule the FreeLang+ General font could not be written.

**The exception to that rule** is a font that is its sources' own, so that their running text shapes, which no other font's budget could afford. Noto Serif Tibetan (a Recommended Script's font, with its layout: 1,880 glyphs for 207 characters, 34,386 lookup bytes) and Noto Sans Mongolian share one: the rule would cut Mongolian to its character map (288 glyphs), and it keeps its layout (3,145 glyphs for 170 characters, 7,608 lookup bytes), so its letters join and its variation selectors choose forms. Together they need 41,992 of the 65,535 bytes and 5,045 glyphs. Noto Sans Duployan is alone in the fifth font, with its layout too (9,522 glyphs for 143 characters; 57,192 lookup bytes, 87% of what a table reaches, for 344 lookups of 5,387 subtables). HarfBuzz shapes sample words of both as the whole source fonts do.

**Each source is whole in one font, with two exceptions**: Plangothic P2's characters of other scripts than the CJK ones are in the FreeLang+ General font, its CJK ones in the CJK font; and Unicodia Funky is cut in two subsets. Its four combining parentheses (U+1AC1 to U+1AC4, marks of Latin phonetic text) are in the Essential font, where their anchors are carried onto Noto Sans's letters, dotted circle, and other marks (see *Marks across sources*); the rest of it stays in the FreeLang+ General font. The script's `ELSEWHERE` table names such a part (a source, the other font, the characters), and *language_font.json* gives the source two rows.

**Each character is in one font, with one exception**: each of the four fallback fonts also has the characters of its own family name (11 to 17 characters: Latin letters, the space, the plus, and the fourth name's en dash; 12 to 18 glyphs with *.notdef*), copied from Noto Sans with the character map alone, so that a font list that draws a family's name in the family shows it whole. Text never takes them from there, since the Essential font comes first. A side effect: the four claim Latin in their OS/2 ranges and code pages.

**The two limits are checked for each font before it is merged**: its glyphs (65,535), and what its lookups need within reach of 16-bit offsets, counted for `GSUB` and for `GPOS` as the plainest arrangement that always packs (each lookup followed by one extension record per subtable). Past either, the build stops with a sentence that names the font and its three heaviest sources, and writes nothing; from 90% of either, it warns and records the warning. A table a little past the count may still pack (the three-font Essential did), but only after HarfBuzz and then fontTools have tried for minutes, so the build refuses it. **When a font nears a limit**, move its heaviest source (the build names it) to a font of its own: one entry in the script's `FONTS` table (label, family, file, PostScript name, the sources it names or a rule, whether its sources keep their layout whole, whether it is optional), one more pair in `LIST_FONT_FALLBACKS`, one line in the installer's list, and the tests' lists. Today the Duployan font is the nearest (87%), and it holds one source that will not grow.

Rebuilding (after *checks/font_characters.json* changes, or a source, or the table of fonts): *--stage plan* (it names each source's font, and says which keep their character map alone and which keep their layout whole), *--stage subsets --seconds 70* until every subset is made, then *--stage merge --font &lt;label&gt;* once for each font, each a command of its own (it keeps the merged font in the work folder and writes nothing), then *--stage merge*, which merges whatever is not merged yet, sets the one line, writes the five fonts, the license file, and *language_font.json*, removes a font file no entry of the table names, and prints the table of fonts. *--repin* is needed only when a source was added or a pin moved. On 2026-11-13 the plan took 0.14 GB of memory at most, the subsets 0.31 GB (40 seconds for all 180), and each merge 0.19 to 0.27 GB and under ten seconds as a command (one to three seconds of merging), since every table packs through HarfBuzz at once; a second build with every subset and every merge made again (*--fresh*) gave the same bytes for the five fonts, the license file, and *language_font.json*. A table that does not pack does not fail at once (HarfBuzz's packer refuses it and fontTools tries its own for about eight minutes before `OTLOffsetOverflowError`), which is why the limits are checked first and why each merge can be run alone: let a slow one finish, and start nothing beside it.

#### Marks across sources

Each character comes from one source, and no source's positioning knows another's glyphs, so the build carries the anchors across. After the merge, Andika's eight letters become bases of Noto Sans's mark lookups (the marks above and the marks below), and Unicodia Funky's four parentheses become marks of them (on every letter of Noto Sans, on its dotted circle, and over its other marks), each with the anchor its own font gives it. A class of marks is found again in the other font by its characters (the acute's class is the marks above), only where most of them agree; and since two fonts put a mark's anchor at different places from its ink (Andika hangs its marks below from 0.049 em under the baseline, Noto Sans from the baseline), each carried anchor is moved by that difference, measured on the marks both classes have. So an acute on ꟍ stands as high over it as Andika's own would, and a parenthesis sits beside a letter as it does in Unicodia Funky. What is not carried: a mark of another script's font on these letters, and Unicodia Funky's parentheses on letters that are not Noto Sans's (Greek and Cyrillic are; Armenian or Georgian are not), where the mark is placed by its outline.

Only what is in Noto Sans's own font can be carried, which is why the four parentheses are a second subset of Unicodia Funky in the Essential font. As HarfBuzz places them (units of a 1,000-unit em): on *a* (ink from 46 to 480 across, to 545 up), U+1AC1 stands at the upper left (ink −15 to 84 across, 630 to 866 up) and U+1AC2 at the upper right (459 to 558); U+1AC3 and U+1AC4 at the lower left and right (−291 to −55 up); on *H* they rise above the capital (808 to 1,044); on the dotted circle they sit within 50 units of where Unicodia Funky itself puts them on its own; on Cyrillic *а* and Greek *α* likewise; over an acute they rise clear of it (860 to 1,096), and under a dot below they drop under it (−467 to −231).

#### How the fonts are made

*checks/train/language_font.py* makes the fonts from 187 fonts (SIL Open Font License 1.1), 174 Noto fonts and the 13 others above: Noto Sans for Latin, Greek, and Cyrillic, a Noto Sans or Noto Serif font per script (Noto Serif Tibetan, Tangut, Toto, Old Uyghur, and Nyiakeng Puachue Hmong among them), Noto Sans Math, Symbols, Symbols 2, and Mono for the character sets' symbols, and Noto Sans KR, SC, and JP for Hangul, the ideographs, and kana. It downloads them at pinned commits of notofonts.github.io (the static TrueType fonts) and google/fonts (the variable TrueType builds of the CJK fonts and of Noto Emoji, instanced at Regular), and at pinned releases or commits for the others (the release archives of SIL's fonts and of Noto Serif Dives Akuru on GitHub; Unicodia Funky, Daarage, Shafarik, and BabelStone Pseudographica, each with its license file, from Unicodia's repository at one commit), outside the repository, stops when a file's or an archive's SHA-256 is not the one *language_font.json* recorded, and checks that each one's name table declares the OFL (Unicodia Funky's in its copyright record, the font having no license record) and that its license reserves no name it uses (the CJK fonts reserve *Source*; Andika and Kanchenjunga reserve their names and *SIL*). No outline is converted but Daarage's (above): every other source has TrueType outlines, and a font with another kind, or with bitmaps, stops the build. Each character comes from its script's font (a punctuation mark or a combining mark: from the font of the texts it is in, so marks attach to their letters; an ideograph: from the font of the writing its rows are in, Noto Sans SC for the Chinese orthographies, JP for Japanese's kanji, and KR for Korean's hanja), then from the general fonts, then from any other Noto font (a script's own font from elsewhere, Kanchenjunga, after them), then from the fonts added on 2026-11-13, then from Noto Emoji, Andika, and Unicodia Funky, in that order, then from Padauk Book, Shafarik, and BabelStone Pseudographica. Who supplies a character is settled before any font of the five is thought of, so moving a source to another font changes no glyph. Each source is subset to its characters, unhinted, and scaled to 1,000 units per em where it is drawn on another, and the subsets of each font are merged into a static TrueType font renamed *FreeLang* and what it holds, so it never stands for an installed font, with a format 12 character map for the characters past U+FFFF beside the format 4 one, and no table but the thirteen a shaped TrueType font needs (`GDEF`, `GPOS`, `GSUB`, `OS/2`, `cmap`, `glyf`, `head`, `hhea`, `hmtx`, `loca`, `maxp`, `name`, and `post`). Its name records are the Windows ones (UTF-16) and the Macintosh ones (Mac Roman, which has the en dash; a font with a name that encoding lacked would get none).

A source that keeps its layout keeps every feature but the vertical ones, which nothing here uses: conjuncts, reordering, marks' positions, and contextual forms, with the glyphs they reach. Sixteen never keep it: those that draw isolated characters only (Noto Sans Math, Symbols, Symbols 2, Mono, Cuneiform, Egyptian Hieroglyphs, Anatolian Hieroglyphs, Noto Serif Tangut, Noto Music, and Noto Znamenny Musical Notation), and the last ones looked in (Noto Emoji, Andika, Unicodia Funky, Padauk Book, Shafarik, and BabelStone Pseudographica), whose positioning knows nothing of the other fonts' glyphs (their anchors are carried instead, as above) and whose alternates nothing reaches. The largest subsets are Noto Sans KR (2.5 MB: the 11,172 Hangul syllables, the jamo, and Korean's hanja), Noto Sans Duployan (1.6 MB), Noto Sans Cuneiform (0.8 MB), Noto Sans SC (0.7 MB), Noto Serif Tibetan (0.6 MB), and Noto Sans Egyptian Hieroglyphs (0.6 MB).

The sources that fit least were looked at again on 2026-11-13, and left as they are, since no better Noto font exists for any of them. Noto Sans Mono draws 146 characters (118 of *Box Drawing*, 24 of *Block Elements*, and 4 of *Miscellaneous Technical*), which no other general Noto font has, and which are a typewriter's by nature. Five Noto Serif fonts draw the scripts that have no Noto Sans (Tibetan, Tangut, Toto, Old Uyghur, and Nyiakeng Puachue Hmong); Noto Serif itself supplies nothing. The fifteen emoji are in Noto Emoji's own style, and Noto's only other emoji fonts are color fonts.

HarfBuzz shapes every run of what the lists and tooltips show with the merged font and with its source's subset, and, for Mongolian and Duployan, which no language's text drew from when this was measured (Mongolian is SLDR's Mongolian's script since 2026-11-14), three sample words each. They agree but for seven runs in the Essential font, none a fault of the merge: a lone zero-width joiner and non-joiner, three other names in Devanagari that hold one (the merged font hides it behind its space glyph, where the lone subset, which has no space, drops it), and two malformed Devanagari sequences, on which the merged font shows the dotted circle the lone subset lacks. The four other fonts differ in none. 26 runs (a Tibetan mark on Noto Sans's dotted circle) are cut across two fonts, with the positions one font gives them. *checks/language_font/language_font.json* lists the characters drawn and not drawn, each font's size, glyph count, lookup bytes, tables, and line, and each source's version, SHA-256, characters, glyphs, lookup bytes, and bytes; *FreeLang_LICENSE* carries the sources' copyright lines and their licenses.

### How it compares with Hyperglot's own checker

On twelve open-licensed corpus fonts (Noto Sans, EB Garamond, Gentium Plus, Poppins, GFS Didot, Noto Sans Arabic, Devanagari, Thai, Khmer, JP, KR, and Amiri), Font Check's base-level support (no composing) and Hyperglot 0.8.2's checker (`Checker.get_supported_languages`, no shaping, draft validity and up, living and constructed languages) agree on 283 to 624 languages per font, and differ in three ways only, each by design:

- Letter-and-mark clusters with no single character (Hausa's *r̃*, Marshallese's *m̧*): Hyperglot without its shaping checks counts them when the font has the letter and the mark; Font Check asks for an anchor that joins them (about 50 languages in each Latin font with all the combining marks). Counting every mark as attached, and Hyperglot in its decomposed mode, the two agree on Noto Sans and Gentium Plus (673 and 658 languages) but for the next two points.
- Languages whose orthographies Hyperglot asks for together (`preferred_as_group`: Serbian in Cyrillic and Latin, Japanese in kana and kanji, Ainu, Pontic): Font Check lists each orthography under its own script, so a Latin-only font covers Serbian's Latin orthography.
- Hyperglot's decomposed mode also takes a modifier letter from its base letter (*ᶿ* from *θ*, by NFKD); Font Check does not, since a modifier letter needs a glyph of its own.

## Vertical metrics

The values are read from FontLab: the current master's ascender, descender, x-height, cap height, and line gap (`flPackage.ascender_value` and its siblings), and the font's OpenType line metrics from its font info (`fgPackage.info`: `openTypeOS2TypoAscender`, `openTypeOS2TypoDescender`, `openTypeOS2TypoLineGap`, `openTypeHheaAscender`, `openTypeHheaDescender`, `openTypeHheaLineGap`, `openTypeOS2WinAscent`, `openTypeOS2WinDescent`, and bit 7 of `openTypeOS2Selection`, *Use Typo Metrics*). An ascender, descender, or win value of 0 reads as not set. Every glyph's ink is measured in every master (its outline as FontLab draws it, components included); for a font with one master, the view's sentences say *in the single master*.

The checks follow Google Fonts' vertical metrics guidance ([gf-docs, VerticalMetrics](https://github.com/googlefonts/gf-docs/tree/main/VerticalMetrics)). In short:

- Windows cuts off whatever reaches above winAscent or below winDescent, so those two should be the family's highest and lowest points, over every glyph of every master. With Use Typo Metrics on, Windows spaces lines by the typo values instead, so the win values can grow to stop clipping without loosening the lines.
- macOS spaces lines by hhea, Windows (with Use Typo Metrics) by typo: the two sets should be equal.
- Line gaps should be 0: applications add a line gap above the line, browsers split it above and below, and Windows without Use Typo Metrics ignores it, so a gap spaces lines differently everywhere.
- For Latin, Greek, and Cyrillic, the line (ascender plus descender) should be 120% to 130% of the em, the default leading of page layout applications; taller scripts (Devanagari, Arabic, Vietnamese's stacked marks) may need more.
- The line's ascender should clear *À*: some macOS applications place the first line by the top of *À* or the ascender, whichever is taller.
- Capitals centered in the line (the room above the cap height equal to the room below the baseline) help web designers center text in buttons.
- An update of a family already released keeps its line spacing, so documents set in it do not reflow: when Use Typo Metrics was off, the old win values become the typo values, and the win values then grow to the new ink.

The view has four parts.

- **The diagram**, across the top: the font's three tallest and three deepest glyphs by ink (each in the master where it reaches farthest, its name and master under it) on one baseline, the one reaching highest and the one reaching lowest always among them and captioned so, with how many other glyphs reach the same value in whole units (*highest (and 1 other)*, *lowest (and 2 others)*, *highest and lowest* for one glyph that is both; image 215, `extreme_notes`), and the metrics as lines across: winAscent, the typo and hhea ascenders, the cap height, the x-height, the baseline, the typo and hhea descenders, and winDescent, labeled at the right edge with their values; equal values are one line with both names (*typo and hhea ascender 965*). Ink past winAscent or winDescent is red: Windows clips it. A bracket at the left spans the line (ascender minus descender, plus the line gap) and gives its share of the em (*120% of the em*). The suggested values that differ are dashed in the highlight color. A glyph selected among the clipped ones is drawn in it too, in the highlight color.
- **The values**: typo ascender, descender, and line gap, hhea ascender, descender, and line gap, winAscent, winDescent, and Use Typo Metrics, now and as suggested, the suggested ones in bold where they differ. Under them, the strategy (*Write suggested values* at the right of the window's bottom row, *Put back old values*, *Copy suggested values*, and *Open Font Info* at its left, while the tab is shown), worded for the font's own designer. *Line spacing [120]% of the em*, chosen to start with, suggests the line spacing at the share set, capitals centered in it, raised to the top of *À* and lowered to the deepest descender of *a* to *z* (and Greek and Cyrillic), with line gaps of 0, hhea equal to typo, win at the ink's extremes, and Use Typo Metrics on; a font whose letters are mostly of another script (Arabic, Devanagari, Han) has the line shared as its ascender and descender are, then raised and lowered to clear its letters' ink. This reproduces the guidance's worked example exactly (typo 965 and −235, win 1116 and 320). *Keep the font's current line spacing*, for an update of a font already released, keeps the typo values if Use Typo Metrics was on, or else takes the old win values as typo values (the share of the em is then grayed out). The rows' tooltips give the reasons.
- **The checks**, beside the values: one line each, a drawn icon (an amber warning, a gray dot for what is good to know, a green check for what passed, in a quieter color) and a short sentence: *Windows clips 15 glyphs at the top, up to 82 units (circumflex.case)*, Use Typo Metrics off, hhea and typo values differing, line gaps (*Line gap 100: set it to 0 and add it to the ascender and descender*), the line's share of the em, *À* above the line's ascender, capitals not centered, the glyphs reaching past the line's ascender or descender (the farthest 40 each way: they may touch the line above or below, which tall diacritics often do), and win values far past the ink. The checks that passed are listed too, so the user sees what was checked. Each line's tooltip has the full sentence; clicking a line about glyphs names them in the log, each a link that opens it in a Glyph window, and selects those Windows clips below (a glyph past the line's descender is not clipped). The checks stand right of the values, their heading (the list's own header) on the line of the values' header, as tall as the values and scrolled when longer, with the strategy under both.
- **Clipped on Windows**, under a line (a heading after a table never reads as its caption): a wrapping row of small pictures of the glyphs Windows clips, the worst first, the part cut off in red and the overflow in units under each. With several masters, *Master* shows every master's (each glyph once, in its worst master) or one master's. Double-clicking a picture opens the glyph in a Glyph window; right-clicking flags it. The row is hidden when no glyph is clipped.

The line's share and centered capitals are judged only for a font whose letters are mostly Latin, Greek, or Cyrillic.

The diagram spans the window, and the values and checks sit under it: six glyphs side by side with their labels need the window's width, and beside the values they would come out about a quarter as tall.

*Write suggested values* (with the count of values that differ: *Write 5 suggested values*) writes those values into the font info, for the whole font (`fgPackage.info`): the typo values (ascender, descender, line gap), the hhea values (the same three), the win values (floats), and *Use Typo Metrics* (bit 7 added to `openTypeOS2Selection`, a list of bit numbers). Only the values that differ are written; each is read back, the log lists each value old and new (*Typo ascender 800 → 965*) and any FontLab did not keep, and the view is checked again. FontLab's *Edit > Undo* does not take the font info back (tried live: it reverted *Use Typo Metrics* only), so FreeLab keeps the values it replaced: *Put back old values* writes them again, into the font written to only, while it is the current one (told apart by `fgPackage.id`, the same through every wrapper of one font: two unsaved fonts of one family name share their path-less key). A value that was not set before stays as written, since no way to unset a font info value from a script is known: the log names it, to clear in *Font Info*. A value FontLab refuses to take back stays held, and the button tries it again. The masters' own metrics (`ascender_value`, `descender_value`, `xHeight_value`, `capsHeight_value`, `lineGap_value`: the current master's, which FontLab draws its guides with) are not part of the suggestion and are never changed. A win, typo, or hhea ascender or descender of 0 reads as not set, so a suggested 0 where the font has none counts as the same. *Copy suggested values* copies them as text, and *Open Font Info* opens FontLab's Font Info (`flItems.requestFontInfoDlg`).

## Masters

Masters interpolate only where every glyph has the same structure in each. Each master is compared with the one chosen in *Compare every master with* (the font's default master to start with), glyph by glyph, and each difference names the glyph, the master, the contour (numbered from 1, in FontLab's order), and what differs:

- a glyph the master lacks;
- the number of contours, and contours in another order (the same node counts, shuffled);
- a contour open in one master and closed in the other;
- the number of nodes in a contour, and the first node whose type differs (on-curve, off-curve);
- a contour's direction (the sign of its area), also when the reversal shows first as node types in another order; a contour whose area is under 5% of its box's (a figure eight, whose halves cancel out) is not judged;
- a contour's start point: with the same nodes and types, the contour's points (centered and scaled to its size) are compared with the reference's at every start the node types allow; when another start fits at least four times as well as the one in place, and the one in place fits poorly, the row says how many nodes on or back the start sits. This is a judgment from the shapes: a contour whose shape changes a lot between masters (a dot drawn as a square in one weight and as a diamond in another) can look moved when it is not. A closed contour whose node types differ but match from another start (a start moved from a corner to the end of a curve) is a moved start too, at the start that fits best;
- components, by glyph name, and their order;
- anchors, by name.

The view has three parts.

- At the top, a chip per kind of problem with the number of glyphs that have it (*Node counts 3*, *Anchors 15*, *No layer 9*), all shown to start: each shows or hides that kind of problem in the list, and a glyph left with no kind shown leaves it. A chip shown is filled in the highlight color, a chip hidden is a gray outline: the chips carry a stylesheet of their own drawn from the palette, as FreeLab's tab bars do, and dark mode leaves them alone (Windows' own style draws a checked chip light whatever the palette, which dark mode garbles).
- At the left, one row per glyph with problems, the worst first (those that cannot interpolate: a missing layer, contour or node counts, node types, contour order, an open contour; then directions, start points, and components; then anchors): its picture, its name, a symbol per master in the font's order (a ring for the master compared with, red where the glyph cannot interpolate, a dashed square where it has no layer, amber where it interpolates oddly, a faint dot where all is well), and whether Font Check can fix it: *Fixable*, *Partly fixable*, or *Needs the designer*. A row it can fix has a check box, ticked to start. In the window's bottom row, *Fix ticked glyphs* at the right (with the count: *Fix 3 ticked glyphs*) and *Fix this* (the selected glyph) at the left.
- At the right, the selected glyph in the master compared with and in another (*Beside it*: the first master with a problem to start with, each master with problems saying how many), side by side at one scale: the contour at fault in the highlight color, its nodes numbered from 1 and its start point ringed, every contour's number at its start when their count or order differs, and anchors as small crosses with their names (those one master lacks in the highlight color); a master without the glyph shows a dashed empty frame. Under the pictures, the problems in that master, one sentence each, and what Font Check can set right and what needs the designer.

### Fix

FontLab users often wish FontLab would force incompatible glyphs to interpolate. Font Check fixes what has one safe mechanical answer, in every master but the one compared with, which never changes:

- **the contours' order**: when the contours' node counts are the same but shuffled, the master's contours are matched with the reference's, one to one, at the least cost (Kuhn and Munkres' assignment): a contour pairs only with one of its node count, and costs the distance between their centers and between their widths and heights (in their glyph's box), plus one when their node types match in neither direction from any start. They are put in that order within their own shape (FontLab's contours belong to shapes; a contour is never moved from one shape to another, which is left to the designer);
- **a contour's direction**: the contour reversed (`flContour.reverse()`, which keeps its first node first);
- **a contour's start point**: the start set (`setStartPoint`) on the node that fits the reference's start, judged as the check judges it (above), and only on an on-curve node of a closed contour (FontLab moves a start onto on-curve nodes only; a start that would fall on an off-curve node, or an open contour's, is left to the designer). After a reversal the start is found again;
- **the components' order**: the master's components put in the reference's order (the same names taken in turn), the other shapes left where they are.

Contour and node counts, node types, open contours, other components, missing layers, and anchors need the designer: the row says so, the row's tooltip and the sentence under the pictures name what is left, and neither *Fix ticked glyphs* nor *Fix this* touches them. A glyph with both kinds has what can be fixed fixed. Each contour is fixed only when the result matches the reference's node types: a contour whose types differ whatever its direction and start is not reversed or moved either.

Every fix is planned from the outlines first, carried out, then read back and compared with the plan's result; the master compared with is read back too. A master whose shapes share their data with the reference's (FontLab's shared elements, by `shapeData.id`) is skipped, since the reference would change with it. The fixes of one click make one step of *Edit > Undo*; the masters are compared again and the comparison drawn again, and the log says what was fixed (*o in Bold (contour 1 reversed, its start point moved 1 node on)*), what was not and why, which glyphs still need the designer, and any other glyph with new problems (an element shared with a fixed glyph).

A font with one master says there is nothing to compare. The outlines are read through FontLab's own objects (`layer.shapes`; a shape's `isComponent` and `shapeData.componentGlyph`; its `transform` and `contours`; a contour's `nodes()` and `closed`; a node's `x`, `y`, and `type`; `layer.anchors`), the shape's transform applied, as Auto Kerning's `layer_path` draws them. Components' positions and scales, node smoothness, and guides are not compared: they interpolate whatever their values.

## How it was checked

The checks and what the views show (the characters to add and their ranking, the languages a ticked set completes, the diagram's lines and labels, the clipped glyphs, the check lines, the masters' marks) sit above the Qt line and are unit tested under Python 3 and 2.7 (*tests/fontcheck_tests.py*). The dialog is run on fakes of PythonQt and of a two-master FontLab font (every Qt call accepted and ignored; its contours, shapes, and layers edited as FontLab's were seen to edit, and `reorderShapes` read each way it might), *Fix ticked glyphs*, *Fix this*, and *Write suggested values* included, which exercises its code paths but not Qt's or FontLab's answers. FontLab's Qt 5 and PythonQt can still differ, so the calls below are to be looked at live.

- **Coverage**: compared with Hyperglot's own checker on twelve corpus fonts, as above. The lists' clicks (a first click, a second on the same row, another row, a heading, a family's arrow and name, a double-click, an empty part), the tabs' switching, the shared checks, and the captions are run on the fake dialog with the selection stood in for and the rows and widgets recording what they are told, so what Qt sends for each click, and how it draws, is still to be looked at live.
- **The other sources' orthographies**: unit tested on the data file read whole (Hyperglot's languages first and unchanged, no language from two sources, each row's source and evidence, South-Eastern Ju checked against a font without its click letters, capitals and marks, every added language under a family) and on the train script's parts with no download (the exemplar sets' syntax, Google's text format, the shape of the characters, the counted text's rule on a text made for it: the floor of 1 in 2,000 and of 3 places, the 1,000 letters, the script, the capitals); the tooltips' source line, the *inferred* mark, the status not given, and the check box that leaves the inferred languages out, in the functions and on the fake dialog. - **The languages list's font**: under Qt 5.15 (PyQt5, Windows' own font database, no window shown), every language label, script heading, and bundled *Missing* text was laid out in *FreeLang* alone: no run taken from another family, no box; a picture of a sample of rows was looked at (Cyrillic, Indic, Southeast Asian, CJK, Tibetan, and rare scripts). With the earlier family list (*MS Shell Dlg 2*, then the font Font Check bundled before this one) Qt 5.15 did not show the wide Cyrillic spacing seen in FontLab's Qt 5.14, so that cause is not proven; one family claiming Cyrillic leaves Qt no other font to pick. *tests/language_font_tests.py* reads the font (no fontTools) and checks it has every character bundled and every character of the list but those left out, is five static TrueType fonts (each of the four optional ones read where its file is there) named apart from Noto that claim their writing systems, share one line, and each stay under a size bound, takes from the fonts other than Noto's only the characters listed, and has its license beside it, and that Font Check finds it beside the script or in *language_font/*, registers it once, sets the list in it at 1.25 times the size (or only enlarges it without the file), and never raises; that a tooltip names the family, that every language's tooltip (for a font with no character at all) is drawn by it but for the characters left to the system, that the families' names are, and that the pictures of the missing characters use it.
- **Vertical metrics**: run on 169 open-licensed corpus fonts (variable fonts at their default and at each axis's ends, standing in for masters), and compared with what fontbakery's checks say of the same fonts (*win_ascent_and_descent*, *os2_metrics_match_hhea*, *linegaps*, *use_typo_metrics*): the same verdict for every font and check but two, Jua and Playpen Sans Hebrew, whose *head* table's box (control points) reaches past winDescent while their ink does not; Font Check measures the ink. In 22 variable fonts only a master other than the default clips, which a check of the *head* table cannot see.
